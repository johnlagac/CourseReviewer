Innovation, technology and the SDGs

FramingSDG submission · 20%
The idea, in plain English
Technology never lands in an empty room. It lands in somebody's actual life, with their politics, budgets and habits already in it.

The course opens by refusing the engineer's daydream — that a good enough solution wins on merit. It doesn't. The world has spent somewhere between $4.5 and $6 trillion on foreign and humanitarian aid since 1946 and the recipients still rate the agencies serving them about 3 out of 10 on meeting their priority needs. Money and good intentions were never the bottleneck. Understanding the people was.

You're askingWhat problem is worth solving, and for whom?
It weighsTechnical possibility against real-world constraint.
The course thesisInnovation need not be human-centered — but it must be human-informed.
The professor's own framing, worth quoting back

"How I will teach this course will be highly human-centered, creating positive outcomes for both business, society and self… however, innovation need not be human-centered, just human-informed."

That distinction is doing real work. Human-centered means the human is the organising principle of the design. Human-informed means evidence from humans shapes a decision that might be driven by something else entirely — a regulation, a cost ceiling, a piece of infrastructure. A reviewer answer that treats the two as synonyms has missed the point of the sentence.

Where the SDGs came from, and why the origin matters

The Sustainable Development Goals did not appear from nowhere. They replaced something that failed for a reason you can name.

 Millennium Development GoalsSustainable Development Goals
How they were madeBy committee, top-down — in the professor's words, "mostly white people in ivory towers"Wide public consultation
Evidence baseExpert judgementMY World Survey (9.7M people); UNDG "A Million Voices" (1.7M people across 190 countries)
Adopted200025 September 2015, by 193 UN Member States
Horizon15 years15 years — to 2030
Structure8 goals17 goals, 169 targets
Why the consultation is the whole point

The SDGs are meant to influence budgets, policies and social norms of countries, cities and organisations. That authority rests entirely on the claim that they came from asking people rather than from telling them. Strip out the consultation and they are just another list.

The Grand Bargain — what happens when trust runs out

A worked case in why delivery constraints beat good intentions. By 2016 donors and aid agencies were locked in a trust deficit over how funds were actually used. The Grand Bargain was the quid pro quo: donors would give more flexible funding, agencies would become more transparent and push more money directly to people in need.

The panel identified three interdependent problems:

  1. Reduce the needs — fewer people requiring assistance in the first place.
  2. Mobilise additional funds — through traditional or innovative mechanisms.
  3. Improve efficiency — get more out of each dollar that does arrive.

The report's blunt line, and the one most worth carrying into your own work: "Individuals and institutions need to move beyond their preoccupation with organizational survival."

The trap

The Grand Bargain is not a funding mechanism and it is not a treaty. It is a reciprocal commitment — each side gives something to get something. Describing it as "a UN fund" or "an aid target" misses what makes it interesting: it is an attempt to fix an incentive problem, not a money problem.

Three flavours of technology innovation

The professor's own taxonomy, and a useful sanity check on any venture idea — including your own capstone.

FlavourWhat it looks likeThe risk it carries
Technology looking for a use caseYou have a capability and need a business case for itThe classic solution-in-search-of-a-problem

Recap
  • Human-informed, not necessarily human-centered. Evidence from people must shape the decision; people need not be the organising principle.
  • The SDGs' authority comes from consultation — 9.7M in MY World, 1.7M in A Million Voices — which is exactly what the MDGs lacked.
  • The Grand Bargain is a trust problem, not a funding problem. Reducing needs, mobilising funds and improving efficiency are its three legs.

Business Model Canvas; TAM, SAM, SOM

Core frameworkGroup submissionSome arithmetic
The idea, in plain English
Nine boxes that force you to say, on one page, who pays you and what it costs you to serve them.

The Business Model Canvas is not a planning document — it is a shared language. Its value is that a CFO, an engineer and a marketer can look at the same page and argue about the same thing. The course leans on it precisely because it makes the economics visible: revenue on the right, cost on the left, and nowhere to hide if one is bigger than the other.

You're askingDoes this business actually hang together?
It comparesWhat you earn against what you spend to earn it.
Start atCustomer segments — block 1, always.

The nine building blocks, in the order you fill them

#BlockThe question it answersWhat people get wrong
1Customer SegmentsWho are you creating value for?Listing "everyone". Segments can be direct or indirect, and come in functional, social and emotional types.
2Value PropositionWhat benefit do they get?Describing the technology. A value proposition is always stated in relation to a segment — it helps that customer get a job done.
3ChannelsHow do you reach them?Forgetting channels run both ways — how they reach you matters as much as how you deliver.
4Customer RelationshipsHow do you acquire, retain and grow them?Confusing it with channels. Four themes: type, bond, intimacy, lifecycle.
5Revenue StreamsHow do they pay you?Treating it as an input. It is an outcome of blocks 1–4.
6Key ResourcesWhat must you have?Listing everything. Only the absolute essentials — tangible and intangible.
7Key ActivitiesWhat must you do?Confusing with resources. Ask: where do we really need to excel?
8Key PartnershipsWho helps you?Ignoring that each partner has their own business model. Look for win–win.
9Cost StructureWhat does it cost to run?Treating it as an input. It is an outcome of blocks 6–8.
Say it in plain words

Blocks 5 and 9 are the only two you do not choose. They fall out of the other seven. If you find yourself "deciding" a revenue stream without having changed a segment, a value proposition, a channel or a relationship, you are guessing.

The worked example: Jollibee

The professor builds the canvas block by block on a company everyone in the room already understands. Learn it in this form — it is the fastest way to check whether you have the blocks straight.

Notice what Jollibee teaches you

Entrepreneurs appear as a customer segment with their own value proposition ("an established brand and system"), their own relationship (franchisor–franchisee) and their own revenue stream (franchise fees). One company, two quite different businesses on one canvas. That is the canvas earning its keep — and a very likely discussion question.

How to actually use it
  • Don't write on the canvas — use Post-Its, so it can change.
  • One idea per Post-It. No bullet points on a Post-It.
  • Colour-code, and use visuals as well as words.
  • Watch your granularity, and distinguish present from future.
  • One idea per canvas. A canvas covering two business models is covering neither.
The technology nuance

You are supposed to start at customer segments. In technology ventures you are often constrained before you begin — by what the platform can do, what data you can get, what you already built. The professor's point is that in tech, blocks 1, 2, 6 and 7 (segments, value proposition, key resources, key activities) shift in relative importance, and the honest canvas admits which constraint came first.

TAM, SAM, SOM — sizing the prize

Three nested circles. Each one narrows the claim, and each narrowing is where the credibility is won or lost.

TAM — Total Addressable Market everyone who could conceivably need this SAM — Serviceable Available Market the slice you could serve with your model and reach SOM — Serviceable Obtainable Market the slice you could realistically win
The two directions, and what each proves

"Top-down tells you the market exists. Bottom-up tells you why you are the right person to crack that market." Top-down means desk research — the library, industry reports, a survey. Bottom-up means your own usage data, your MVP, your actual unit economics. A third route, value theory, leans on domain experts and focus groups — the professor's own name for it is the "trust me bro" approach, which tells you how much weight to give it alone.

The Goldilocks zone

Investors read these three numbers as a test of judgement, not of arithmetic. Both directions of error are fatal, and they fail differently.

 Too high, and they think…Too low, and they think…
TAMToo much competition to surviveThe market is simply too small
SAMDid you even understand the industry? Do you expect competitors not to punch back?The market is too niche
SOMDo you have a secret sauce, or are you high?Why are you competing at all — is this worth doing?

The worked narrative: a bank for refugees

The professor builds a sizing story from the top down, deliberately, so you can watch a plausible narrative assemble itself.

  1. Establish the scale. More than 60 million international refugees have no access to credit or banking. Counted as a population, the world's forcibly displaced would rank about 12th among countries — between Japan (123M) and the Philippines (114M).
  2. Narrow to a serviceable market. Turkey and Germany host the most international refugees — roughly 6 million between them. Start there.
  3. Find the money already flowing. UNHCR alone disburses USD 10.248 billion a year in refugee support, and Germany pays asylum seekers about EUR 670 per month each.
  4. Now build the financial product. Sounds good — so what are the problems?
Annual stipend per asylum seeker in GermanyEUR 8,040

That last step is the lesson. Every number above is real and the story is coherent, and none of it establishes that refugees can legally hold an account, that the stipend can be routed through a private product, or that you can run KYC on someone without documents.

The trap: TAM, SAM, SOM is not a business

A good market size proves an opportunity, never a sustainable business. The professor's standing example is peer marketplaces — "always funded, always failing" — which produce beautiful TAM/SAM/SOM slides and tight narratives precisely because everyone can picture the market, while the hard operational problems stay hidden. If your answer stops at the market size, you have not answered.

What investors are actually reading

"They don't really care what the actual answer is. They want to see how you arrived at that number." — Helen Min, Phenomenal Ventures. Show the working. A defensible method beats a flattering total.

Recap
  • Revenue streams and cost structure are outcomes, not choices — they fall out of the other seven blocks.
  • A value proposition is always stated for a specific segment. Jollibee's franchisees get a different one from its diners.
  • Market size is an opportunity, not a business, and the method matters more than the number.

Jobs to be Done

Core frameworkUsed in every later topic
The idea, in plain English
Nobody wants a drill. They want a hole — and actually they want the shelf up before their partner gets home.

Jobs to be Done says that people "hire" a product to make progress in a particular circumstance. Christensen's sharpest claim is the one students skip: the circumstances matter more than the customer's characteristics. Demographics tell you who someone is. The job tells you what they were trying to do at the moment they reached for something — which is the only thing you can design for.

You're askingWhat progress is this person trying to make?
It replacesDemographic segmentation and feature lists.
Written fromThe customer's perspective, never the company's.
The definition to be able to quote

"Job" is shorthand for what an individual really seeks to accomplish in a given circumstance. JTBD is a lens revealing the circumstances — or forces — that drive people and organisations toward and away from decisions.

"The circumstances are more important than customer characteristics, product attributes, new technologies, or trends." — Christensen, Hall, Dillon & Duncan, HBR, 2016

The statement, and why the shape matters

When [Situation] I want to [Motivation] So I can [Expected Outcome]

Worked: "When I am running, I want to listen to music, so I can be motivated."

Each slot does a job of its own. Situation is the circumstance that triggers the need — change it and the job changes. Motivation is what they want to do, not the product they use to do it. Expected outcome is the progress they are actually after, and it is the slot most people leave vague.

A JTBD should be customer-focused, discrete and centred on a desired outcome. "Jobs describe the underlying human needs, not the features of the product." — Sunita Mohanty, Product Lead, Meta.

The same situation, two different jobs, two different businesses

Watch how changing only the motivation slot sends you to a completely different product.

Job statementWhat it points toGrowth type
"When I'm drilling a hole, I want to do it quicker and cleaner, so I can do other things"Laser guides, dust removersSource of improvement
"When I'm drilling a hole, I want to do it cheaply, so I can spend more money on other things"A tool rental marketplace, double-sided tapeSource of expansion / diversification
Why this is the whole framework in one table

Double-sided tape is not a better drill. It is a competitor that a drill manufacturer would never see coming, because it only appears once you state the job rather than the product category. That is what "focus relentlessly on the customer" buys you.

The three kinds of job

TypeThe questionExample
FunctionalHow do customers want to solve their problem?Get from point A to point B by air
SocialHow do they want to be perceived?Be seen as someone who flies a premium carrier
EmotionalHow do they want to feel?Arrive rested and unflustered
The class example worth remembering

The functional job of every airline is identical — "point A to point B from the air". So why would anyone pay almost double to route through Hong Kong to reach Taiwan? Why choose Cathay over Cebu Pacific, over Philippine Airlines, over EVA?

Because the functional job was never the one in contention. Starlux launched into a Taiwanese duopoly in 2020 against Cathay, ANA, JAL and Korean Air — and its founder, the ousted EVA chairman Chang Kuo-Wei, is a working pilot who still flies his own customers. The professor's question is the point: what advantage does that give him in identifying jobs to be done?

The classic: NyQuil → ZzzQuil

NyQuil sold for decades as a cold remedy. Then someone noticed consumers taking a couple of spoonfuls to help them sleep — when they weren't sick at all. ZzzQuil was born: the good night's rest they wanted, without the active ingredients they didn't need. Understanding the job let an existing product become a new one, with no new chemistry.

JTBD is not How Might We — know which you are writing
 Jobs to be DoneHow Might We
Whose perspectiveThe user'sThe provider's — "you"
What it gives youDiscrete and tacticalA direction to take

Both are useful; they are not interchangeable, and swapping them is a standard way to lose marks. See topic 7 for How Might We.

Recap
  • Circumstances beat characteristics. The situation someone is in predicts behaviour better than who they are.
  • When / I want to / So I can — and the outcome slot is the one people leave vague.
  • Functional, social and emotional. The functional job is usually the one everyone already serves; the other two decide who wins.

The Web of Abstraction

Core frameworkTwo directions
The idea, in plain English
Ask "why?" until you reach something too broad to build, then ask "what's stopping you?" until you reach something too big to fix. The useful work is everything in between.

It sits on S.I. Hayakawa's Ladder of Abstraction — the observation that language runs from the concrete and specific up to the abstract and general. The Web of Abstraction turns that into a brainstorming tool: start from one functional job and walk in both directions to surface the social and emotional jobs above it and the obstacles below it.

Ladder up"Why?" and "Why else?" — find the jobs behind the job.
Ladder down"What's stopping you?" and "What else?" — find the barriers.
Always start atA functional JTBD.

Laddering up — from a task to a motive

Take the Airbnb job: "Find a room in a foreign city." Ask why, and why else, repeatedly.

Find a room in a foreign city ← functional JTBD ├ why? To attend a business conference │ └ why? Build new relationships · Expand my perspective ├ why else? To immerse myself in a new experience │ └ why? I'm bored with my current situation └ why else? I was ordered to, but within a budget └ why? To look for new opportunities

Now the professor's question lands: why Airbnb and not Hilton? Nothing in the functional job answers that. Everything in the layer above it does.

The B2B version — where it gets uncomfortable

A sales manager using CRM tools. Functional job: "Identify prospective customers."

Identify prospective customers → Increase the number of customers our sales team can approach → Increase market share of our product → It is the primary metric for my performance review → Because I want to get promoted → I want my parents to see me as successful
Read what just happened

The top of that ladder is not a business requirement. It is a person's self-image. The professor's split is explicit:

  • The bottom of the ladder gives you key functional features — it is a functional job, so build the function.
  • The top gives you the social and emotional jobs, which tell you what else the product needs. Can it easily generate the report that makes this manager look good in their review? That feature does not appear anywhere in "identify prospective customers".

Laddering down — from a task to a barrier

Same Airbnb job, opposite direction. Ask what is stopping them, and what else.

Find a room in a foreign city ← functional JTBD ├ what's stopping you? Unsure of safety │ ├ Can I trust the host? · Is it clean? · Is it a safe area? │ └ I don't know them · I heard horror stories └ what else? I don't have the budget ├ My boss only gave me a budget └ I don't have the cashflow right now
What ladder-down output is for

Same split, different use. The concrete barriers become features. The emotional ones — "I heard horror stories" — become the things your marketing must hit. Reviews, verified hosts and a guarantee are not product decisions and messaging decisions separately; they are the same insight pointed at two teams.

Generating barriers when the obvious ones run out

Use impairments and limitations deliberately: lack of resources, disabilities, and the constraints of an organisation in terms of governance or approvals. "Leadership doesn't give me the budget" is as real a barrier as anything in the product.

When to stop — in both directions
  • Up: stop when it becomes obvious or too broad. "To be happy" is where the ladder dies.
  • Down: stop when the problem is too big, too broad, or has no obvious solution.
  • Keep going until you reach jobs you don't actually solve — that gap is the output, not a failure.
Rules for the worksheet
  • Describe individual jobs — resist grouping them into one compound sentence.
  • Write from the customer's perspective, not your company's.
  • Keep the outcome the customer wants top of mind.
  • Defer judgement, strive for quantity, and ideate in different directions — you may ask more than five whys.
Recap
  • Up gives you motives, down gives you barriers. Up = "why / why else", down = "what's stopping you / what else".
  • The concrete end becomes features; the abstract end becomes positioning and marketing.
  • Jobs are not static. Changes in the landscape or in social norms create new jobs, new challenges and new opportunities.

Growth Opportunity Framework and RIME

Portfolio viewScoring grid
The idea, in plain English
"Innovation" covers both repainting the kitchen and moving to another country. Say which one you mean before anyone signs off on it.

The Growth Opportunity Framework — Nagji and Tuff's Innovation Ambition Matrix — sorts initiatives by how far they move from what you do today. Its purpose is not classification for its own sake: knowing where an idea sits tells you its risk and its expected return, which is what lets you hold a portfolio rather than a pile of bets.

Two axesHow new the customer is, how new the experience is.
It tells youRisk, return, and how defensible the position is.
You can startAnywhere — but own the risk that comes with it.

The three zones

ZoneMoveRiskWho and what
CoreImproveLowExisting customers, incrementally improved experience
AdjacentExpandMediumNew customers with the same experience, or the same customers with a new experience
TransformationalDiversifyHighNew customers and a new experience
The counter-intuitive bit

Low risk is not the same as safe. The professor's warning: it is harder to defend against competition in the core, precisely because a low-risk incremental improvement is low-risk for your competitors to copy too. The lower-left corner feels comfortable and erodes quietly.

How it connects to JTBD

Overlay the two and you get the three growth questions: What other jobs can you address with the experience you already offer? Do you observe customers using your product for a different job than you designed for? Can you expand into adjacent jobs? Dyson shipped its first branded vacuum in 1993 — the framework is how you answer "where do you go now?"

RIME — choosing between opportunities

Once the Web of Abstraction and the growth framework have produced more opportunities than you can fund, RIME scores them on four axes.

AxisThe questionHow to measure it
ReachHow many people does this touch?Size of the addressable group
ImpactHow well does it solve their job?Your JTBD should already be measurable. If it isn't: customer satisfaction, time spent, effort
MonetizationWhat does it earn?Which gives the largest margin; which has the highest willingness to pay
EffortWhat does it cost to do?Money, time and resources — and which leverages what you already have

The class exercise. Score these four Uber opportunities on Reach, Impact, Monetization and Effort — then say which one you'd fund:

Package delivery · Food delivery Car ridesharing · Expand to Indonesia

And the second set, for a camera company: an integrated camera on a drone, a handheld camera stabiliser, eBikes, an action camera. The eBike is in that list on purpose.

The point of scoring Effort separately

Reach, Impact and Monetization all describe the prize. Effort is the only axis that describes you — and specifically, how much of the work you have already done. Two companies scoring the same opportunity will get identical R, I and M, and different E. That difference is your actual strategy.

Recap
  • Core / Adjacent / Transformational = improve / expand / diversify = low / medium / high risk.
  • Adjacent means one thing changes — new customer or new experience, not both. Both at once is transformational.
  • RIME = Reach, Impact, Monetization, Effort, and Effort is the axis that differs between you and your competitor.

The three audits: Journey, Friction, Competency

Applied tools
The idea, in plain English
Two of these audits look outward at where the customer struggles. One looks inward at what you are already good at. You need both halves before you commit.

By this point the frameworks have told you what job the customer is hiring you for and which opportunities are worth chasing. The audits are where that becomes concrete: where exactly does the experience break, why does it break, and do you have any business fixing it?

Journey AuditWhere do they drop off?
Friction AuditWhat makes them stop?
Competency AuditWhat can we actually do?

Journey Audit — following the customer end to end

Audit the journey from first discovering your product through to actually receiving its benefit. Three stages, and the job changes at each one.

Discovery→Action→Benefit
StageWhenSpotify — "I want to listen to music anytime and anywhere"
DiscoveryPre-purchaseFree trial for premium · sharing on social media · available on iOS, Android and web
ActionPurchaseA free tier · easy to purchase
BenefitPost-purchaseMusic discovery · shareable playlists · beyond music to podcasts and audiobooks
The trap

Remember the situation in the job statement — the job may be different at different points in the journey. Auditing a single touchpoint tells you about that touchpoint. It tells you nothing about whether someone abandoned you two steps earlier.

Friction Audit — Adam Alter's three phases

  1. Exploratory. If you watched your customer using your product, what stopping cues do they confront?
  2. Intervention. At each touchpoint — is there a way to do this that required less energy and effort from the customer?
  3. Confirmation. Do your interventions actually improve how customers interact with the product?
Friction is not bad service

"Customer friction is created by flaws in either the product design or the purchase and delivery processes. Poor customer service is marked by flaws in execution." — Joe Procopio. Fixing friction means redesigning something. Telling staff to try harder is addressing a different problem.

Competency Audit — the inward-looking one

Design Thinking: the process and How Might We

Core frameworkNames the course
The idea, in plain English
Five modes, not five steps. In a poster they run left to right; in real work you keep falling back to the previous one, and that falling back is the method.

Design Thinking got its name almost by accident. Tim Brown was searching for a phrase beyond "design with a small d" when David Kelley — Stanford professor, founder of IDEO — mentioned that every time someone asked him about design, he found himself adding the word thinking to explain what designers actually do. The term stuck. Stanford's d.school (the Hasso Plattner Institute of Design) was founded in 2005; Brown's TED talk "Tales of Creativity and Play" followed in 2008, with its thesis that playfulness is what allows us to be creative.

You're askingHave we framed the right problem before solving it?
It integratesHuman needs, technical possibility, business viability.
Its shapeLooped and recursive, never linear.
The definition worth memorising

"Design thinking is a human-centered approach to innovation that draws from the designer's toolkit to integrate the needs of people, the possibilities of technology, and the requirements for business success." — Tim Brown, IDEO

The five modes

ModeWhat it does

IdeateGenerate many options

Why "modes" and not "steps"

"Linear in posters. Looped, recursive and overlapping in real life. You will keep revisiting Empathize. You will keep reframing in Define after a Prototype you built turns out to be misunderstood. That is the work." Anyone describing Design Thinking as a five-step sequence you complete once has described a workshop, not a method.

How Might We — the question that opens the problem

A How Might We statement is written from the provider's perspective and gives a direction rather than a specification. Four parts:

How Might We [verb] [user] [process] [benefit] How might we make neighborhood communities communicate better to reduce community hate?
The calibration problem — the examples the professor uses
StatementVerdict
"How might we solve world peace?"Too broad — nothing to design against
"How might we stop my neighbour from singing karaoke at 4am so I don't kill them?"Too narrow — one person, one solution already implied
"How might we make neighbourhood communities communicate better to reduce community hate?"About right — broad enough to allow many answers, narrow enough to know when you have one

"Broad enough but not too narrow" is the whole test. And note the pattern: the too-narrow version already contains its own solution, which is the more common failure in practice.

HMW versus JTBD — the distinction that gets tested

How Might We is the provider asking what direction to take. Jobs to be Done is the user's account of what they are trying to accomplish, and it is discrete and tactical. You write the HMW to open a space; you write the JTBD to know what success inside that space would look like. See topic 3.

The honest critique — four limitations

  1. There are successful companies that don't practise Design Thinking at all. It is not a precondition for good products.
  2. It needs heavy, real user involvement — which may simply not be available to you.
  3. It is not a one-day workshop. Done properly it takes several months.
  4. It carries systemic limitations and built-in biases that lead into traps.
The professor's sharpest reservation

"Design Thinking on its own is not biased for action and implementation, where innovation technology execution is often a function of execution more than anything else. It is great for research and human-centric design strategy, but a bit lacking in the effective implementation department."

And two framings that sit alongside it: Design Thinking is part of human-centered design, not the whole of it. And human-centered design must also account for human self-centeredness — people optimise for themselves, and a method built on asking them nicely has to allow for that.

Design Thinking × the SDGs

Design Thinking was exploding around 2009; the SDGs were ratified in 2015. Human-Centered Design was front and centre exactly while the goals were being drafted — which is part of why the consultation in topic 1 looks the way it does. The class question is what each did to the other.

Recap
  • Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test — modes, not steps, and you will revisit all of them.
  • HMW = verb + user + process + benefit, broad enough to permit many answers and narrow enough to recognise one.
  • It is strong on framing and weak on execution. Knowing that is what stops you using it for the wrong half of the problem.

Test — design the conversation, not the demo

The 80% rule

"If your test session is mostly you talking, you are pitching, not testing. The user should be doing 80% of the talking. The prototype should fade into the background."

  1. Set context. Explain it's a draft. Tell them you want their honest reaction — and that you didn't make it.
  2. Hand it over. Let them read or try it without instruction. Stay silent for the first 60 seconds.
  3. Probe. What surprised you? What confused you? What's missing? What would be next for you?
  4. Capture. Two columns: works and breaks.
"You didn't make it" is a technique, not modesty

People soften criticism to protect the person in front of them. Distancing yourself from the artefact removes the social cost of saying it's confusing — which is the only feedback worth having.

Four ways to fool yourself
  • Confirmation hunting — going in to validate a solution rather than understand a problem.
  • Sample of some — your team interviews each other. The data is real. Is it representative?
  • Stated preferences — ask what people would do and they lie politely. Behaviour data, not opinion data, is what counts.
  • Note-taking blindness — writing down what fits your hypothesis and skipping what didn't.
The mindsets underneath the methods
  • Curiosity — ask the obvious question; stay with what you don't yet understand.
  • Tolerance for ambiguity — the first frame is rarely the right frame.
  • User empathy as discipline — not a feeling, a practice. Re-anchor in the data.
  • Show the work — visible artefacts; decisions made on what's there, not what someone remembers thinking.
  • Radical collaboration — diverse people in one room. The expert is rarely the user.

AI in Design Thinking: the LLM-Delphi

Research-basedThought Leadership material
The idea, in plain English
The experts you most want to survey are exactly the people too busy to answer. So what if the panel were synthetic?

This topic is built on live research the professor brought to class — Bertolotti & Mari (arXiv:2502.21092, Feb 2025), Rony, Ciarli & Nomaler (working paper), and Calleo & Pilla's "Real-Time AI Delphi" (Futures, 2025). The question is narrow and testable: can LLM agents stand in for a panel of human experts, and how would you know?

You're askingCan we get expert-quality foresight without the experts?
The division of labourDT as generation, Delphi as validation.
The honest verdictAugmentation, never replacement.

What a Delphi actually is

Not a poll — this distinction is the whole topic

"A Delphi is not a poll — it's a structured, iterative elicitation where experts answer in private, see the anonymous distribution of others' answers, and revise. The aim is not forced consensus but calibrated, surfaced disagreement: outliers can hold their position so long as they can justify it."

Recruit→Round 1→Feedback→Round 2+
RecruitDomain experts across geographies, dispositions and sectors
Round 1Anonymous answers, plus reasoning text on each item
FeedbackThe distribution is shown back; outliers are asked to justify
Round 2+Experts revise. A stable distribution is the result
Where the name comes from, and where it is used

Conceived in the 1950s by Olaf Helmer and Norman Dalkey at RAND, named for the Oracle of Delphi. Its largest user by a wide margin is healthcare and clinical guidelines — international panels writing treatment guidance almost always use a Delphi-style mechanism. Also common in education and curriculum design, and in government foresight: most EU member states run national Delphi exercises to shape research and innovation programmes.

Why nobody runs the Delphi they wish they could

BarrierThe reality
RecruitmentReal domain experts have day jobs. Response rates of 5–15% are typical even with incentives
Coverage600+ item × task cells would need thousands of experts. Costs run through the roof
TimeTwo or three rounds of asynchronous review takes months, sometimes a year
RefreshBy publication the technology or situation has already moved

The synthetic alternative

Classic DelphiLLM-Delphi
Panel: 15–50 human expertsPanel: N LLM agents with structured profiles
Months of asynchronous roundsHours of automated rounds

A persona here is not a character to role-play

"A persona is a structured profile of attributes that conditions the LLM's response. Not a character to roleplay — a constraint set the model uses to anchor its judgements." The art is enough structure to differentiate respondents, not so much that you bury what you're asking about.

{ "role": "robotics engineer", "sector": "automotive mfg.", "country": "Germany", "years_experience": 12, "education": "MSc Mech. Engineering", "view_on_automation": "pragmatic" }

Crucially, the agents do not see each other in round 1. They see anonymised peer output from round 2 onward — mirroring the privacy-then-feedback structure of the classic method.

Three scenario families the synthetic panel surfaced:

  • Tech silos — regional ecosystems harden into separate model lineages, datasets and standards; interoperability erodes.
  • Digital iron curtain — geopolitical blocs become technical blocs; which model you may use is a function of where you are.
  • Tech cold war — GenAI as a strategic-asset domain: export controls, infrastructure rivalry, talent capture. Innovation slows where rivalry hardens.

The agents were asked about geopolitical tensions, economic disparities, regulatory frameworks and ethical considerations — deliberately open-ended, long-horizon, contested. "The point of a Delphi is not what is the answer but where do informed minds disagree, and why."

What it does well, and what it doesn't

What the working paper actually found
  • Works well: broad, tangible technologies match human distributions. Mid-weight open models (DeepSeek, Kimi, Llama-class) are sufficient for most cells. Variance and entropy stabilise after 5–6 repetitions per persona, making cost predictable.
  • Be careful: accuracy drops on detailed, niche technologies — though note human accuracy drops there too, where N is small. Blind prompting without temperature tuning produces unreliable swings. Top-tier proprietary models cost real money at full survey size.

The line to carry into the Thought Leadership piece

"LLM-Delphi cannot replace going into someone's kitchen at 2pm to watch how they study for an exam. But it can validate, refine and pressure-test the JTBD you write after that visit, when visiting multiple kitchens is logistically hard." The right division of labour is Design Thinking as generation, Delphi as validation.

The three discussion questions — worth having an answer to
  1. If the interview or test is composed entirely of LLMs, whose biases are you actually reading — the model's, the prompt-writer's, or the prompt-template's?
  2. Is "no human ground truth" a feature (forecasting where data is impossible) or a fatal flaw (no way to know you're wrong until it's too late)?
  3. Where would relying on this tool be quietly irresponsible?
Recap
  • A Delphi seeks calibrated disagreement, not consensus — private answers, then anonymised feedback, then revision.
  • The barrier it solves is recruitment: 5–15% response rates, months of rounds, results stale on arrival.
  • Treat synthetic output as hypothesis, not validation. Agents have no skin in the game.

Lean Startup: MVP, build–measure–learn, pivots

Core frameworkFive principles
The idea, in plain English
A startup exists to learn how to build a sustainable business. Not to make stuff, not to make money, not even to serve customers — to learn.

Eric Ries defines a startup as "a human institution designed to create new products and services under conditions of extreme uncertainty." The consequence is the whole method: you cannot plan your way out of uncertainty, so you experiment. Every untested assumption is a "leap of faith", and the job is to test the riskiest ones cheaply, fast.

You're askingIs this business model viable at all?
Its engineBuild → Measure → Learn, as fast as possible.
Its outputValidated learning, not vanity metrics.
Where it sits relative to the canvas

"The Lean Startup model offers a methodology for testing and iterating on the Business Model Canvas you have listed down." The canvas is the hypothesis; Lean Startup is how you find out whether it is true. Steve Blank (Lean) and Alexander Osterwalder (BMC) are, as the professor puts it, the two fathers of the MVP.

The five principles

#PrincipleWhat it means
1Entrepreneurs are everywhere"Not just in startups; they are in big companies, governments and non-profits." You don't need a garage.
2Entrepreneurship is managementA startup is an institution, not just a product — so it needs management, of a new kind geared to its context. "Even a pirate ship needs a good quartermaster."

The line worth quoting in a submission

"'Simple' is good. 'Incomplete' is not." — Jason Cohen. What is great for the product team can be bad for customers, and what is bad for customers is ultimately bad for the company. By 2026, 25 years on, the professor's framing is to reiterate the core meaning of MVP while naming the false prophets who use the term for performative goals. No rules are universal; context is key.

Two pitfalls

PitfallWhy it bites
Moving fast and breaking thingsAt what cost? When we build dams or power plants there is associated regulation, because we know the cost of building one. Software shipped the same way without the equivalent reckoning
The feedback loop to doomListening to customers too much. You may be hearing their concerns without hearing what is actually needed — usage ≠ solving a problem

Pivot versus persevere

The definition, and the eight types

Pivot = a structured course correction in strategy that keeps the vision. Persevere = the data validates the current path, so push harder. The decision should be made by evidence, not ego — though the professor is candid that the real world often works the other way.

Pivot types: zoom-in · zoom-out · customer segment · customer need · platform · channel · technology · business model.

Worked case: Copia Global (Kenya), founded 2012.

  1. Hypothesis. People in rural Kenya would order consumer goods from a catalogue.
  2. The lean experiment. Instead of building a warehouse and delivery fleet, they printed a few catalogues and gave them to local kiosk owners.
  3. The finding. Kiosk owners were bad agents — they saw the catalogue as competition.
  4. The pivot. Switch to other community influencers as agents — a move made possible only by their flexible, low-cost start.

Test with 10–20 people and you can fail small and fail fast before wasting millions. Organisational flexibility is the ability to adapt to new information without high switching costs — and pivoting requires the organisational permission to change direction when the data says so.

Is Lean dead?

Steve Blank's own answer, 2018: "Lean was an answer to a specific startup problem at a specific time… It's a response to scarce capital, and when that constraint is loosened…" — the sentence trails off on purpose. A method built for capital scarcity behaves differently in a capital glut. Hold that thought for topic 14.

Recap
  • A startup is an institution operating under extreme uncertainty — so experiment, don't plan.
  • The MVP maximises validated learning per unit of effort — and the 2017 critique says make it Simple, Lovable and Complete instead.
  • Pivots keep the vision and change the strategy, and should be triggered by evidence.

Blue Ocean Strategy and non-disruptive innovation

Strategy
The idea, in plain English
If the market is a fight over a fixed pot, the smartest move may be to stop playing that game rather than to play it better.

Kim and Mauborgne of INSEAD started from a research question: markets are often zero-sum — one wins at the other's expense — but what if I don't want to play that game? Their answer was to avoid red oceans (existing markets, existing players) and create blue oceans (no players), competing on value innovation rather than on differentiation or cost leadership.

Red oceanContested market, zero-sum, compete on price or difference.
Blue oceanUncontested space, created rather than fought over.
The moveChange the game, don't win the existing one.

The evidence that motivates it

A study of business launches in 108 companies:

Type of launchShare of launchesShare of revenueShare of profit
Line extensions — incremental improvements to existing offerings86%62%39%
Aimed at creating new markets14%38%61%
New-market launches produced this share of profit from 14% of launches61%

Fourteen percent of the launches produced sixty-one percent of the profits. That asymmetry is the entire argument for looking outside the red ocean.

The context it was born into — worth knowing

Blue Ocean was published in 2005, into a world of Jack Welch's retirement and Six Sigma, Michael Porter's Competitive Advantage, Jim Collins' Good to Great and The Innovator's Dilemma. Note the dates: Design Thinking (2009) and Lean Startup (2011) did not exist yet. Blue Ocean is answering a strategy question in a period when the available answers were all about competing harder.

How it evolved

YearAdditionWhat it addresses
2005Blue Ocean StrategyThe original red/blue distinction and value innovation
2015Red Ocean TrapsBlue turns red — oceans do not stay uncontested
2017Blue Ocean Shift / "humanness"People are inherently afraid of change
2024Non-disruptive innovationCreating new markets without destroying existing ones
Non-disruptive innovation — the 2024 turn

"Managers should recognise that when it comes to innovation and growth, disruption is not the only path." The shift is from a military, war-based mindset — where the goal is to win territory from competitors — to an abundance mindset, where the only limitation is the scope of your imagination in creating new value. Note how far this sits from Christensen's disruption framing, and that it is a deliberate correction rather than an accident.

The trap

"Blue ocean" is not a synonym for "new product" or "no competitors right now". It is specifically value innovation — raising buyer value and lowering cost at the same time, rather than trading one against the other. An expensive premium product with no current rival is not a blue ocean; it is a differentiation play waiting for a competitor.

Human-centred × Blue Ocean — the takeaway the professor draws

"You don't have to play the game of the market — go ahead and change the game." And note the trajectory: as Blue Ocean evolved it focused more and more on the humanness of the players (executives and employees), the market (customers) and society, rather than on pure market dynamics. That is what connects it back to Design Thinking.

Recap
  • Red ocean = contested and zero-sum; blue ocean = created. The play is value innovation, not differentiation or cost leadership.
  • 14% of launches, 61% of profits. New-market creation is rare and disproportionately profitable.
  • Blue oceans turn red (2015), and by 2024 the framework argues disruption is not the only path to growth.

Positioning — April Dunford

The definition

"Positioning defines how your product is a leader at delivering something that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about." And the negative definition matters as much: positioning is not messaging, not a tagline, not your brand story, not your vision or mission.

How a startup should handle positioning over time
  1. Keep it loose initially. You are still figuring out what works.
  2. Tighten once patterns emerge — when you have enough traction to see them.
  3. Use the "bowling pin" strategy. Start with one specific niche — the lead pin — to build success and referenceable customers, then expand to adjacent segments.
  4. Then be consistent. The common mistake is losing focus or changing messaging too often. Great companies hold consistent messaging over long periods.

  • Four competitor types — narrow, form, need, resource — and the dangerous ones are the last two.
  • Positioning is not messaging. Loose at first, tightened on evidence, then held steady.

Why recurring revenue earns a higher multiple
  • ARR is contracted, not earned — less uncertainty, lower risk premium, higher multiple.
  • Compounding growth — existing customers expand, so ARR grows without new sales. The best kind of growth.
  • Lower perceived CAC risk — subscription customers are retained rather than re-acquired, so acquisition cost amortises over a longer lifetime.

Skill-biased technical change — and why it may be running backwards

The definition

"Skill-biased technical change (SBTC) is a shift in production technology that favours skilled labour over unskilled labour by increasing the productivity and therefore the relative demand for skilled workers." The hypothesis is that technological advance is not neutral. It has been a key explanation for rising wage inequality.

SourceContribution
John Hicks, The Theory of Wages (1932)The broader idea of "biased" technical change — innovation can favour one factor of production over another rather than being neutral
Zvi Griliches (1969)Capital–skill complementarity: capital equipment is more complementary with skilled labour than with unskilled
Autor, Katz & Krueger (1998)"Computing Inequality" — the rising college wage premium and the computer/skilled-worker link
Card & DiNardo (2002)The critique: "A narrow focus on technology has diverted attention away from many interesting developments in the wage structure that cannot easily be explained by SBTC"
The methodological lesson — this is the transferable bit

"A theory that explains something with broad strokes can completely fail to explain the specifics. SBTC could explain 'inequality went up while computers spread' — but it couldn't explain the timing, or which groups were specifically affected. It did have impact, but it wasn't a direct line." Which is exactly why you should think critically about current AI-and-labour claims.

PatternClaimEvidence
Inverse skill biasGenerative AI benefits less-skilled workers moreBrynjolfsson's experiments — "AI tended to close the gap between the most-experienced and less-experienced workers." Some call-centre agents in the study were in the Philippines

"If you're a junior worker, AI may help you perform like a senior. But if you're a recent graduate trying to get that first job, the door may be closing." The two effects together mean AI may equalise productivity within roles while widening inequality between insiders and outsiders. As the deck puts it: "the easiest people to fire are the ones you have not hired yet" — if AI disproportionately targets entry-level tasks, the bottom rungs of career ladders erode.

The Turing Trap

"When AI augments human capabilities, enabling people to do things they never could before, humans and machines are complements. In contrast, when AI replicates and automates existing human capabilities, machines become better substitutes for human labour and workers lose economic and political bargaining power." Humans still excel at exception handling and multi-modal context.

Recap
  • Cash is king, margin is queen — margin buys the right to experiment and be wrong.
  • ARR = MRR × 12, and recurring revenue earns a higher multiple because it is contracted rather than earned — which is also why it is easier to massage.
  • SBTC may be running in reverse with generative AI — inverse skill bias within roles, seniority bias between them.

Blitzscaling, financialization and the Long Tail

Cautionary
The idea, in plain English
Three different ways a technology company can destroy itself — by growing too fast, by forgetting how to build, or by misreading where the money in a market actually sits.

This topic is the course's sceptical core. Each of the three ideas was, at its peak, presented as an unambiguous good. Each has a body count.

BlitzscalingSacrifice efficiency for speed, deliberately.
FinancializationMoving money around instead of making things.
Long TailNiches in aggregate beating the hits — sometimes.

Blitzscaling

The definition, in Reid Hoffman's own terms

"Blitzscaling means that you're willing to sacrifice efficiency for speed, without waiting to achieve certainty on whether the sacrifice will pay off. If classic startup growth is about slowing your rate of descent as you try to assemble your plane, blitzscaling is about assembling that plane faster, then strapping on and igniting a set of jet engines while you're still building the wings. It's do or die."

Drew Houston of Dropbox on what that growth feels like: "It's like harpooning a whale. The good news is, you've harpooned a whale. And the bad news is, you've harpooned a whale!"

The hangover — and why it matters now

"Our world is still reeling from the hangover from blitzscaling and SoftBank's Vision Fund. Be careful of lessons, habits and advice from this era."

And blitzscaling is back: Hoffman has created Blitzscaling Ventures to fund AI startups, and the World Economic Forum now writes about "blitzscaling for impact" to build impact unicorns. The professor's counterweight is blunt — OpenAI reportedly spent $8.7 billion on inference on Azure alone in the first three quarters of 2025, more than double the $3.7 billion in 2024. "Now might be the only time when running AI was cheap, just like ridesharing, online food ordering and on-demand streaming were cheap. The gravy train will not run forever."

Connect it back

Recall Steve Blank in topic 10: Lean was a response to scarce capital. Blitzscaling is what the same industry does when capital is abundant. They are not competing theories so much as the same industry under two different funding regimes.

Financialization

The definition

"Financialization refers to the increase in size and importance of finance in the overall operations of a business, often driven by the pressure of the public market through executives incentivised by public-market performance."

Thomas Palley's fuller version: "a process whereby financial markets, financial institutions and financial elites gain greater influence over economic policy and economic outcomes," transforming economic systems at both macro and micro levels — describing the period from roughly 1980 to the present, in which debt-to-equity ratios rose and financial services took an increasing share of national income.

The pattern, as the professor draws it:

  1. Started with strong tech DNA
  2. Stock market and management pressure for "efficiency"
  3. Financialization — buybacks, divestments, cost cuts
  4. Talent drain and innovation decay
  5. Technical failure or irrelevance

The evidence cited: Boeing — "McDonnell Douglas bought Boeing with Boeing's money," followed by the 737 MAX and the Lion Air accident report. Intel — $10 billion spent repurchasing its own shares across 2014 and 2018, cleaning up the books and raising the share price while drying out the assets; by 2025, laying off over 15,000 employees, more than 15% of its workforce.

Why it keeps happening

Two quotes on Jack Welch. Roger Martin: "He was an unsophisticated connoisseur of the idea of shareholder value maximisation who was able to somewhat blindly and without reflection implement it in a powerful company." David Gelles: "Welch single-handedly ushered in a new era of cutthroat capitalism, wrenching corporate America from the postwar years — when there was a real focus on multiple stakeholders including employees and communities — into an age where shareholders and investors came first."

The structural observation: industries with fewer MBAs and less finance tend to be more successful — Silicon Valley is "light on MBAs and heavy on engineers" — and a 2015 comparative study found productivity declines in markets with rapidly expanding financial sectors. The shift is from "making things" to "moving money around".

The line to take into your own career

"Being able to clearly articulate long-term thinking today is more important than ever, especially as your company faces financialization internally or externally. Financialization is easier than doing proper design thinking, research and development and spending the time to build up core competencies. We are lucky now that we can point to carcasses to prove our point."

The Long Tail

Chris Anderson, 2006

"Products in low demand or with low sales volume can collectively build a better market share than their rivals, or exceed the relatively few current bestsellers — provided the store or distribution channel is large enough." That last clause is the whole theory. The internet expanded the channel because stocking and distribution cost fell to near zero; AI is now driving the marginal creation cost down too.

 What it claims
Pareto principleA small share of products drives most of the sales — and its hidden constraint is physics: shelf space, inventory, distribution
Long TailRemove the physical constraint and the aggregate of non-hits can exceed the bestsellers
Where data science actually enters
  • Recommendation systems are long-tail engines. Netflix, Spotify and Amazon use ML to surface niche content. "The algorithm is the shelf space that Anderson said the internet unlocked" — without data science, the tail stays buried.
  • Micro-segmentation. Clustering, cohort analysis and personalisation at scale turn "niche of one" from metaphor into reality.
  • Big data made the tail legible. The cost of understanding low-frequency signals dropped, so rare events — rare diseases, rare fraud patterns, rare segments — became analytically tractable.

And the creator economy is a long-tail product decision: which niche video gets recommended, at what threshold a creator gets monetised, how to balance diversity against engagement.

The Long Tail paradox — two ways it fails
  • The tail problem. Models trained on majority-class data perform poorly on rare classes. Anderson's commercial opportunity is machine learning's hardest technical problem — it spawned imbalanced learning, few-shot learning, zero-shot generalisation and long-tail recognition in computer vision.
  • Re-concentration of attention. More niches and more voices, but filter bubbles and superstar effects compress the tail in practice. Winners are still winners.
Recap
  • Blitzscaling trades efficiency for speed on purpose — and is a response to abundant capital, just as Lean was a response to scarce capital.
  • Financialization follows a five-step path from strong tech DNA to irrelevance, and Boeing and Intel are the named cases.
  • The Long Tail needs a large enough channel, and the algorithm is the shelf space — but attention re-concentrates anyway.

CommunicationGroup submission · 20%
The idea, in plain English
Write the press release before you build the thing. If you cannot make the announcement sound worth reading, you have learned something cheap.

A PRFAQ is a mock press release plus a detailed FAQ, written as if the product has already launched. Its purpose is to shift from an internal company perspective to a customer one. Customers are bombarded with new products daily, so you have to get over the hump of "So what?" — the professor's example being a company announcing "We are launching in the Philippines!" and Filipinos replying: so what?

You're askingWould a customer care about this at all?
It producesAlignment and anticipated objections.
Best forBig bets, 0-to-1 products, relaunches.
The act of writing is the point, not the document
  • The act of writing and reviewing is what matters most — writing clearly, showing reasoning, documenting trade-offs, issues and concerns.
  • It is a thinking and alignment mechanism.
  • It is high-level. It is not a replacement for a Product Requirement Document and carries no execution detail.

"The goal is not to create a document but to create a baseline and limitations for discussion. It is biased for action, and biased that you are expected to launch something — not to do something just because."

How to run a PRFAQ review

  1. Read — in silence, together.
  2. Discuss and ask questions — from the most junior to the most senior.
  3. Debate.
  4. Take notes, and improve the document and the project.
Why most junior first

Same mechanism as the Ideate rules in topic 8: once the most senior person in the room has stated a view, everyone else's question quietly becomes a comment on that view. Ordering by seniority is a structural fix for a status problem.

What to watch for — the professor's own checklist

In the press release:

  • Focusing only on capabilities, not customer outcomes.
  • Saying "users" or "businesses" instead of a specific type of customer.
  • Starting with "We are launching X" instead of "Customers struggle with Y".

In the internal FAQ: asking only the questions you can answer confidently, and avoiding the scary or important ones.

Everywhere: inflated language — superlatives and adjectives that don't survive scrutiny. "Revolutionary." "Seamless." "Best-in-class."

Deadline: before session 19.

  • Write it as though it has already launched, and lead with the customer problem, not your launch.
  • Internal FAQ is money, ownership and alignment; external FAQ is price, access and delivery.
  • The document is a discussion baseline, not a spec — and the review runs most junior first.

Same toolkit, completely different operating system. The frameworks don't change; what changes is what can kill you.

The Startup Way — what Lean Startup left out

Same author, six years later, different reader

Lean Startup (2011) taught the team how to run experiments, but not the company how to fund or govern them. It assumed a small founding team with full decision rights and said nothing about HR systems, legal review, procurement or finance — nor about how to kill a project without firing the people.

The central thesis: the missing function

Modern companies are organised around five functions — engineering/technology, marketing, sales, finance, HR. Ries argues that under extreme uncertainty this list is incomplete. The missing one is entrepreneurship, formally recognised, resourced and governed. Just as finance has a CFO, entrepreneurship needs structural ownership. Otherwise innovation is "everyone's job" — which ought to be true, but also means it is nobody's job to champion it.

Growth Boards and metered funding

  • A Growth Board is a small panel of 4–6 senior leaders.
  • It acts exactly like a venture capital firm — but with corporate money.
  • It reviews internal startups at staged milestones: Seed → A → B → Scale.
  • At each gate it makes one of three decisions: more funding, pivot, or stop.
  • It replaces the annual budget cycle for the innovation portfolio, forcing honest portfolio thinking — most projects will be killed, but the employees will not be.

A healthy internal startup: small (4–8 people), cross-functional (engineering + design + business + data), full-time on one project rather than 20% of five jobs, with permission to talk to customers directly without an account-manager filter, funded in tranches by a special fund rather than an annual budget. The properties are a set, not a menu.

Innovation accounting — three stages
  1. Vanity metrics — pageviews, downloads, LinkedIn likes.
  2. Actionable metrics — activation, retention, time-to-paid, by cohort.
  3. Innovation accounting — did the experiment move the leading indicator?

Traditional accounting — revenue, margin, EBITDA — is useless when there is no product yet.

Two framings that map onto it
