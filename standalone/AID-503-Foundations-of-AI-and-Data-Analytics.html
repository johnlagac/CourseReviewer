Data types and structures

Quiz 1Conceptual
The idea, in plain English
What you are allowed to do to a number depends on what the number means, not on the fact that it is a number.

A jersey number and a salary are both numeric, but averaging jerseys is nonsense and averaging salaries is not. Before any analysis, the first question is always which kind of variable you are holding — because that single answer decides which methods are even available to you.

You're askingWhat kind of variable is this?
It decidesWhich statistical analysis is legitimate.
The rule of thumbQuantitative data gives you more options than categorical.

The two branches

TypeWhat it recordsScalesArithmetic?
QuantitativeHow many (discrete) or how much (continuous)Interval, ratioYes — ordinary arithmetic is meaningful
CategoricalA label or name identifying an attributeNominal, ordinalNo — analysis is "rather limited"
The trap

Categorical data can be numeric. A postal code, a customer ID, a Likert response coded 1–5 — all numeric on the page, all categorical in meaning. The slides say this explicitly: categorical data "can be either numeric or nonnumeric". Deciding by looking at the column is how people get this wrong.

The transactional example

Session 1 puts a Grab Food receipt on screen and asks: what are the variables, and how would you characterise them? That is the exam-shaped version of this topic — you are handed a real artefact and asked to classify what is in it.

Field on the receiptTypeScale
Order total (₱)Quantitative, continuousRatio
Number of itemsQuantitative, discreteRatio
Restaurant nameCategoricalNominal

Say it in plain words

"Numbers you can add up" versus "names you can only count". Everything else follows from that.

Recap
  • Quantitative = how many or how much, always numeric, arithmetic is meaningful.
  • Categorical = a label, nominal or ordinal, and it may well look numeric.
  • The type chooses the method. Quantitative data has more methods available to it.

The four kinds of analytics

Quiz 1Conceptual · high yield
The idea, in plain English
Four questions, arranged along time: what happened, why, what will happen, and what should we do.

This taxonomy is the spine of the whole course — the enterprise sessions use it to grade organisations, and the AI sessions position machine learning as the predictive and prescriptive end of it. If you remember one framework from AID 503, this is the one.

You're askingWhat is this analysis for?
It ordersPast → present → future.
Moving rightMeans moving from information to insight.

The four categories

CategoryThe questionExamples from the deck
DescriptiveWhat happened?Data queries, standard reports, descriptive statistics, data visualisation, cluster analysis, what-if spreadsheet models
DiagnosticWhy did it happen?Drill-down analysis, correlation analysis, cohort analysis, statistical hypothesis testing, root cause analysis
PredictiveWhat will happen?Linear regression, time series analysis, predictive data mining
PrescriptiveWhat is the best course of action?Optimisation, simulation, decision analysis
The trap

Two placements catch people out. Cluster analysis is listed under descriptive, not predictive — it describes structure that is already in the data. And hypothesis testing is diagnostic, not descriptive — it is explaining a relationship, which is the "why" question. Learn the deck's own lists; they are not always where intuition puts them.

Information versus insight

The deck lays the same four questions on a grid with a second axis. Reporting, alerts and extrapolation sit on the information row; modelling, recommendation and optimisation sit on the insight row. The slide's own conclusion:

"To provide information is a pre-requisite to doing analytics but development is needed to move up to insighting."

What analytics is, formally

Davenport et al., Competing on Analytics (2006/7): "the extensive use of data, statistical and quantitative analysis, exploratory and predictive models, and fact-based management to drive decisions and actions"

And the shorter line the deck offers alongside it: analytics is the scientific process of transforming data into insights for making better decisions.

Recap
  • Descriptive → diagnostic → predictive → prescriptive, running past to future.
  • Cluster analysis is descriptive; hypothesis testing is diagnostic. Both surprise people.
  • Information is the prerequisite, insight is the goal — reporting alone is not analytics.

Spreadsheet modelling

Quiz 1ExcelComputational
The idea, in plain English
Separate what you were given, what you get to choose, and what you want to know — then wire them together with formulas.

A spreadsheet model is not a pile of numbers; it is three kinds of cell. Inputs are fixed facts. Decision variables are the levers you control. Outputs are what you actually care about, and they are computed, never typed. Get that separation right and sensitivity analysis, charting and Goal Seek all become free.

InputsGiven, fixed for this model.
Decision variablesWhat the decision maker controls.
OutputsDetermined by the other two.

Goal Seek solves one equation in one unknown — it varies a single input cell to force a single output cell to a chosen value. Probability and expected value

Quiz 1Computational
The idea, in plain English
A probability is a number between 0 and 1 measuring how likely something is — and where that number comes from is itself a choice.

The deck is careful about something most courses skip: there are three legitimate ways to assign a probability, and which one you use depends on what you have. Equally likely outcomes, historical frequencies, or informed judgement. Managers mostly live in the third.

Near 0The event is quite unlikely.
Near 1The event is almost certain.
They must sum to 1Across all experimental outcomes.

The two requirements

0 ≤ P(Eᵢ) ≤ 1 for every outcome i P(E₁) + P(E₂) + … + P(Eₙ) = 1

Three ways to assign them

MethodBasisDeck's example
ClassicalEqually likely outcomes — each gets 1/nRolling a die: each face is 1/6
Relative frequencyExperimentation or historical dataX-ray department waiting times over 20 days
SubjectiveJudgement and experience, expressing degree of beliefTom and Mary pricing the same house offer differently
The trap

Two people assigning different subjective probabilities to the same event are not both wrong. In the deck's example Mary puts the offer's acceptance at 0.8 and Tom at 0.6, and the slide presents both as legitimate — a subjective probability expresses degree of belief. The requirement is internal consistency (each set sums to 1), not agreement between people.

Expected value: the market-return case

An investor sees five scenarios for the national economy in the coming year:

Economic outcomeMarket returnProbability
Rapid expansion23%0.12
Moderate expansion18%0.40
No growth15%0.25
Moderate contraction9%0.15
Serious contraction3%0.08
E(R) = Σ p·R = 0.12(23) + 0.40(18) + 0.25(15) + 0.15(9) + 0.08(3) = 2.76 + 7.20 + 3.75 + 1.35 + 0.24
Expected return15.3%

Note that 15.3% is not one of the five listed returns, and it never has to be. An expected value is a weighted average, not a forecast of what will actually occur.

Recap
  • Every probability sits in [0,1] and the set sums to 1.
  • Classical, relative frequency, subjective — three legitimate sources, chosen by what you have.
  • Expected value weights each outcome by its probability, and need not equal any outcome.

Describing a data set

Quiz 1Computational
The idea, in plain English
Two numbers describe most data sets: where the centre is, and how spread out things are around it.

The mean answers "what is typical". The variance and standard deviation answer "how typical is typical" — whether the data huddles near the centre or scatters widely. Nearly every inferential method later in the course is built on this pair.

CentreMean, or weighted mean.
SpreadVariance, then its square root.
Why the rootTo get back to the original units.

Simple mean

Twelve business graduates' monthly starting salaries sum to $71,280.

x̄ = Σxᵢ / n = 71,280 / 12 = $5,940

The trap

The slide for this table has an OCR-garbled cell — purchase 5's cost renders as "255755". The surrounding arithmetic is unambiguous: 2,600 / 800 = $3.25, and that value makes the column total 18,500 exactly. If you are reading the same slide, use 3.25.

Variance and standard deviation

Sample: s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1) s = √s² Population: σ² = Σ(xᵢ − µ)² / N σ = √σ²

The only structural difference is the denominator: n − 1 for a sample, N for a population.

Say it in plain words

Variance is the average squared distance from the mean. Squaring stops positive and negative deviations cancelling; taking the root afterwards puts the answer back into pesos, pounds or whatever you started with.

Recap
  • Weight by volume when volumes differ — the unweighted mean answers a different question.
  • n − 1 for a sample, N for a population.
  • Standard deviation is the square root of variance, and is in the original units.

Decision analysis and EMV

Quiz 1Computational · conceptual
Which decision trees these are

These are decision-analysis trees — decision nodes, chance nodes, payoffs, probabilities. They are not the CART classification trees of machine learning, which share the name and nothing else. The course outline lists decision trees under session 4; the slides actually teach them here, in session 2, as the graphical form of EMV.

The idea, in plain English
When you must choose before the uncertainty resolves, score each option by its probability-weighted average payoff and take the best one.

You cannot wait to see which outcome happens — that is what makes the decision hard. So you value each option by what it would earn on average if you faced the same gamble many times, and pick the highest. That number is the expected monetary value.

You're askingWhich decision, before knowing the outcome?
It weightsEach payoff by its probability.
Highest EMVIs the preferred decision.

Uncertainty is not risk

UncertaintyRisk
What it isSomething that simply existsDepends on your position
Your controlYou typically have noneFollows from your exposure
The testIf an uncertain event makes no difference to you, there is no risk — though the uncertainty is unchanged.

The six elements of a decision problem

  1. A problem has been identified that requires a solution.
  2. A number of possible decisions have been identified.
  3. Each decision leads to a number of possible outcomes.
  4. There is uncertainty about which outcome will occur, and probabilities are assessed.
  5. For each decision and outcome, a payoff is received or a cost incurred.
  6. A "best" decision must be chosen using an appropriate decision criterion.

Four criteria, and why EMV wins

CriterionWhat it looks atTemperament
Best of the worst outcomesThe worst case for each decisionPessimistic
Best 5th percentileThe lower tail of each distributionRisk-conscious
Best of the best outcomesThe best case for each decisionOptimistic
Smallest varianceSpread of outcomesStability-seeking
EMVProbability-weighted average payoffThe deck's preferred criterion
The trap

The deck states flatly that "the EMV criterion doesn't guarantee good outcomes." Choosing the highest EMV and then losing money does not mean you decided badly — it means the gamble went against you. Exam questions like to probe whether you understand that EMV judges the decision, not the result. The criterion also asks you to value a decision with a given EMV the same as a sure amount equal to that EMV, which is exactly where real risk-averse managers depart from it.

Worked EMV

A vendor chooses between two order quantities. Demand is high with probability 0.6 and low with probability 0.4.

DecisionPayoff if high (0.6)Payoff if low (0.4)EMV
Order large$12,000−$3,000$6,000
Order small$6,500$4,000$5,500
Large: 0.6(12,000) + 0.4(−3,000) = 7,200 − 1,200 = 6,000 Small: 0.6(6,500) + 0.4(4,000) = 3,900 + 1,600 = 5,500
Order large — EMV $6,000

And note what the criterion ignores: ordering large can lose $3,000, ordering small never loses anything. A minimax decision maker picks the opposite way.

The tree

A decision tree is the graphical form of the same calculation, and earns its keep when decisions are sequential — when one choice happens, an outcome is revealed, and then another choice follows. It shows the order of events, the probabilities and the monetary values in one picture.

□ decision node — you choose the branch ○ chance node — probability chooses the branch ◁ terminal — the payoff Solve by rolling back: compute EMV at each chance node, take the best branch at each decision node, work right to left.
Recap
  • EMV = Σ (probability × payoff), and the largest EMV is the preferred decision.
  • A good decision can have a bad outcome. EMV does not guarantee results.
  • Trees are for sequential decisions, solved by rolling back from right to left.

Sampling and point estimation

Quiz 1Computational · vocabulary
The idea, in plain English
You cannot measure everyone, so you measure some and accept that your answer is an estimate with a known way of being wrong.

Point estimation is the simplest form of statistical inference: compute the sample's version of the quantity you want and use it as your guess at the population's. The sample mean estimates the population mean, the sample proportion estimates the population proportion. Everything in topics 8 to 10 is about quantifying how wrong that guess might be.

You're askingWhat is the population value?
You computeThe same quantity in the sample.
You acceptDifferent samples give different estimates.

The vocabulary, which is examinable

PopulationA collection of all the elements of interest
SampleA subset of the population
Sampled populationThe population from which the sample is drawn
FrameA list of the elements that the sample will be selected from
Target populationThe population we want to make inferences about
The trap

The sampled population and the target population are different things, and the deck's "Practical Advice" slide exists entirely to make that point. You want to know about all Filipino consumers (target) but you sampled from a list of mall shoppers (sampled). Whenever they diverge, your inference is about the list, not about the thing you cared about. The slide's requirement is that the two be "in close agreement".

Finite versus infinite populations

A simple random sample of 30 employees is drawn from a firm of 2,500, recording annual salary and whether the employee completed the management training programme.

This table is the whole lesson. The estimates are close but none is exact, and a different random sample would have produced a different set. That gap is what interval estimation exists to describe.

Say it in plain words

An estimator is the recipe (x̄, s, p̄); an estimate is the number it produced this time. Because the recipe is applied to a random sample, the estimator is itself a random variable.

Recap
  • x̄ estimates µ, p̄ estimates p, s estimates σ.
  • Different samples give different estimates — that variability is the whole subject.
  • Target population ≠ sampled population unless you have checked that it is.

Interval estimation

Quiz 1ExcelComputational · high yield
The idea, in plain English
Report a range instead of a single number, and say how confident you are that the range contains the truth.

A point estimate cannot be expected to hit the population parameter exactly. So you add and subtract a margin of error, producing an interval. The margin is built from three things: how spread out the data is, how much of it you have, and how confident you want to be.

You're askingHow close is my estimate likely to be?
Wider intervalMore confidence, or noisier data.
Narrower intervalMore data — n is under the root.
Point estimate ± Margin of error

Interval for a mean — credit card debt

70 households, mean credit card debt $9,312, sample standard deviation $4,007. Build a 95% confidence interval.

x̄ ± t(α/2) · s/√n 9,312 ± 1.995 × 4,007/√70 9,312 ± 1.995 × 478.9 9,312 ± 955
95% CI$8,357 to $10,267

The sentence to write: "We are 95% confident that the mean credit card debt for the population of U.S. households is between $8,357 and $10,267."

Interval for a proportion — women golfers

900 women golfers surveyed; 396 were satisfied with the availability of tee times.

p̄ = 396/900 = 0.44 p̄ ± z(α/2) · √[p̄(1−p̄)/n] 0.44 ± 1.96 × √[0.44(0.56)/900] 0.44 ± 1.96 × 0.01655 0.44 ± 0.0324
95% CI40.76% to 47.24%
The trap

Note which multiplier each case uses. The mean interval uses t (1.995 here, because σ is unknown and estimated by s). The proportion interval uses z (1.96). Reaching for 1.96 on the credit-card problem is the single most common error in this topic — with n = 70 it happens to be close, which is exactly why it goes unnoticed.

How much data is enough

Say it in plain words

95% confidence is a statement about the procedure, not about this one interval. Repeat the whole exercise many times and about 95% of the intervals produced would contain the true parameter. This particular interval either contains it or does not.

Recap
  • Mean uses t, proportion uses z. Check which before reaching for a multiplier.
  • √n is in the denominator — quadrupling the sample halves the margin.
  • n ≥ 30 is the working rule, relaxed if the population is near-normal, tightened if it is skewed.

Hypothesis testing

Quiz 1Computational · high yield
The idea, in plain English
Assume the claim is true, then ask how surprising your data would be if it were. Surprising enough, and you stop believing the claim.

You never prove the null hypothesis. You put it on trial, compute how likely data this extreme would be under it, and either reject it or fail to reject it. That asymmetry is deliberate and it is what most exam traps are built on.

H₀A tentative assumption about a parameter.
HₐThe opposite of H₀ — often the research claim.
Small p-valueData unlikely under H₀ → reject.

The five steps

  1. Develop the null and alternative hypotheses.
  2. Specify the level of significance α.
  3. Collect the sample data and compute the test statistic.
  4. Use the test statistic to compute the p-value.
  5. Reject H₀ if p-value ≤ α.

Which way round do the hypotheses go?

SituationH₀Hₐ
New drug should lower blood pressure more than the existing oneIt does not lower BP moreIt does lower BP more (the research hypothesis)
Bottle label claims 67.6 fl ozThe label is correct, µ = 67.6 (assumption to be challenged)µ < 67.6

The slides are candid that this is the hard part: "correct hypothesis formulation takes time and practice", and sometimes it is easier to identify the alternative first.

Worked test — Hilltop Coffee

The FTC checks Hilltop's claim that its large can contains 3 pounds of coffee, at α = 0.01. A sample of 36 cans gives x̄ = 2.92 pounds; previous FTC testing establishes σ = 0.18.

1. H₀: µ = 3 Hₐ: µ < 3 2. α = 0.01 3. z = (x̄ − µ₀)/(σ/√n) = (2.92 − 3)/(0.18/√36) = −0.08 / 0.03 = −2.67 4. Lower-tail area for z = −2.67 → p-value = 0.0038 5. 0.0038 ≤ 0.01 → reject H₀
p-value0.0038
Reject H₀

The sentence: "There is sufficient evidence at the 1% level to conclude that the mean filling weight is less than 3 pounds."

The trap

This is a one-tailed test, so the p-value is the single lower-tail area, 0.0038. Doubling it out of habit gives 0.0076 — which still rejects here, so the mistake hides. It will not hide on a question engineered to sit between the two. Decide the tail from Hₐ before you touch the table: a "<" or ">" alternative is one tail, a "≠" alternative is two.

What the p-value actually is

The deck's own wording: the probability of the sample given that the null hypothesis is true — the strength of support the data gives the null. Small means reject. It is not the probability that H₀ is true, and failing to reject is not proof that H₀ is true.

Recap
  • Reject H₀ when p ≤ α, and never claim to have proved H₀.
  • The alternative sets the tail count — < or > is one, ≠ is two.
  • The research claim usually goes in Hₐ; a label or standard being challenged goes in H₀.

Comparing two populations

Quiz 1ExcelComputational
The idea, in plain English
Estimate the difference between two groups, and ask whether that difference could plausibly be zero.

The trap

There are two different standard errors in this one worked example and they are not interchangeable. They are numerically close here, which is precisely why swapping them survives a careless read.

Recap

  • "Differ" means two-tailed — double the tail area.
  • A CI excluding zero agrees with rejecting H₀ at the matching level.

Linear regression

Quiz 3Computational
The idea, in plain English
Draw the straight line — or plane — that comes closest to every point, where "closest" means smallest average squared miss.

You're askingPredict a numeric target from features.
It minimisesMean squared error.
ML vocabularyConstant = bias, coefficients = weights.

The model and its objective

With n observations and m features: Y = a + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ + … + bₘXₘ + ε Choose a and the bᵢ to minimise the mean square error: mse = (1/n) Σⱼ [ Yⱼ − (a + b₁X₁,ⱼ + b₂X₂,ⱼ + … + bₘXₘ,ⱼ) ]²
The translation you need

Statistics calls a the intercept or constant and the b's the coefficients. Machine learning calls a the bias and the b's the weights. Same objects, and both vocabularies appear in this course.

Two ways to solve it

MethodWhen
Analytic — invert a matrixStandard approach for ordinary least squares, and for ridge regression
Gradient descentWhen a minimum is required and there is no analytic solution — LASSO, elastic net, logistic regression

Gradient descent, in the deck's five steps

  1. Choose a starting value for the parameters.
  2. Find the steepest slope — the direction in which parameters must change to reduce the objective function most.
  3. Take a step down the valley in that direction.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3.
  5. Continue until you reach the bottom of the valley.
The trap

Which algorithm goes with which method is examinable and easy to mix up. Ridge has an analytic solution. LASSO and elastic net must use gradient descent — the deck says so explicitly on both slides. The reason is that the absolute-value penalty in LASSO is not differentiable at zero, which is also exactly what lets it set weights to exactly zero.

Recap
  • Linear regression minimises mean squared error, and needs relatively little data once linearity is assumed.
  • Bias = constant, weights = coefficients — the ML names for familiar things.
  • Ridge is analytic; LASSO and elastic net need gradient descent.

The dummy variable trap

Suppose you have a constant term and two dummies, v₁ and v₂, where one is 1 exactly when the other is 0.

Y = a + … + b₁v₁ + b₂v₂ + ε For any constant C this is identical to: Y = (a + C) + … + (b₁ − C)v₁ + (b₂ − C)v₂ + ε because v₁ + v₂ = 1 always.

Add C to the constant, subtract C from every dummy coefficient, and the predictions do not change at all. There is no unique solution — infinitely many coefficient sets fit exactly as well. The deck's fix: regularisation solves this problem. The classical fix is to drop one dummy.

The trap, literally

The consequence is that individual dummy coefficients are not interpretable when the trap is present. They can be shifted by any C you like. Reading "neighbourhood 7 adds $12,000" off such a model is reading an artefact of where the solver happened to stop.

The correlation problem

Two features for predicting house price: X₁ = square feet of basement, X₂ = square feet of first floor. In most houses these are nearly equal, so they are highly correlated.

Best fit to the training set might be: Price($'000s) = 10X₁ − 9.5X₂ A better model is something like: Price($'000s) = 0.5X₁ or Price($'000s) = 0.5X₂

The first almost certainly overfits. Because X₁ ≈ X₂, the huge positive and huge negative weights nearly cancel — giving sensible predictions on the training data and wild ones on any house where basement and first floor differ.

How to spot it

Large coefficients of opposite sign on features you would expect to be related, or a coefficient whose sign is the opposite of what domain sense says. The Iowa house-price model in topic 20 shows exactly this — weights coming out negative "when we would expect them to be positive".

Recap
  • One-hot for unordered categories, direct integer coding when a natural order exists.
  • The dummy trap makes the solution non-unique — coefficients shift freely by any constant C.
  • Correlated features give huge cancelling weights that fit the training set and generalise badly.

Regularisation

Quiz 3Computational · high yield
The idea, in plain English
Charge the model a fee for every unit of coefficient it uses, and it will only spend where the fit genuinely improves.

Regularisation adds a penalty on the size of the weights to the thing being minimised. Big weights now have to earn their place. The result is a simpler model that generalises better — and with LASSO, one that has discarded the useless features entirely.

RidgePenalises squared weights.
LASSOPenalises absolute weights.
Elastic netBoth at once.
Before anything else

You must first scale feature values. The deck states this as a precondition, and the baby example uses z-score scaling. A penalty on coefficient size is meaningless when one feature is measured in dollars and another in years — the penalty would fall almost entirely on whichever feature happens to have small units.

The three penalties

MethodMinimiseSolverEffect on weights
RidgeMSE + λ Σ bᵢ²AnalyticShrinks them toward zero
LASSOMSE + λ Σ |bᵢ|Gradient descentEliminates less important features completely
Elastic netMSE + λ₁ Σ bᵢ² + λ₂ Σ |bᵢ|Gradient descentMiddle ground between the two
The one distinction that matters

Ridge shrinks; LASSO selects. Ridge will make a useless feature's weight very small but never exactly zero. LASSO sets it to zero and removes the feature from the model — which is why LASSO doubles as a feature-selection tool. Its name says so: Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator.

Logistic regression

Quiz 3Computational
The idea, in plain English
Run a linear model, then squash its output through a curve that turns any number into a probability between 0 and 1.

Linear regression predicts a number and will happily predict a probability of 1.4. Logistic regression fixes that by passing the linear combination through the sigmoid, which cannot leave the interval (0, 1) no matter how extreme the input. That one change turns a prediction machine into a classifier.

You're askingPositive outcome or negative?
It outputsA probability, not a class.
Fitted byMaximum likelihood, via gradient ascent.

The model

Y = a + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ + … + bₘXₘ (the linear part) Q = 1 / (1 + e^(−Y)) (the sigmoid)

As Y runs from −10 to +10 the sigmoid runs from almost 0 to almost 1, crossing 0.5 at Y = 0. It is asymptotic at both ends, so Q never reaches 0 or 1 exactly.

How it is fitted

Maximise over the training set: Σ ln Q + Σ ln(1 − Q) positive negative outcomes outcomes Equivalently, minimise the cost function: −(1/n) [ Σ ln Q + Σ ln(1 − Q) ] Add λΣbⱼ² or λΣ|bⱼ| to regularise.
The trap

This maximisation cannot be done analytically — the deck is explicit. There is no matrix to invert as there is for ordinary least squares; you need gradient ascent (equivalently, gradient descent on the cost function). Pairing "logistic regression" with "closed-form solution" is a reliable way to lose a mark.

The LendingClub case

Detail
TaskPredict whether a loan is good or defaults

Read the signs, which all make sense: owning your home helps, higher income helps slightly, a higher debt-to-income ratio hurts (the one negative coefficient), and a better credit score helps.

Worked prediction

A borrower who rents (X₁ = 0), earns $50,000 (X₂ = 50.000), has debt-to-income of 29.62% and a credit score of 735 — the second row of the deck's data table, which was in fact a good loan:

Y = −6.1546 + 0.297(0) + 0.0006(50) − 0.0365(29.62) + 0.0115(735) = −6.1546 + 0 + 0.03 − 1.08113 + 8.4525 = 1.24677 Q = 1 / (1 + e^(−1.24677)) = 1 / (1 + 0.28738) = 0.7768
P(good loan)0.777

Above any sensible threshold, so the model classifies this as a good loan — which matches the recorded outcome.

Mind the units

Income enters in thousands. The deck's data column is headed "Income ($'000)" and shows 50.000 for a $50,000 earner. Substituting 50,000 instead of 50 adds 30 to Y and drives the probability to essentially 1 for everyone. The model was fitted on unscaled data, so the units on each feature are load-bearing.

Recap
  • Sigmoid Q = 1/(1 + e^(−Y)) maps any real number into (0, 1).
  • Fitted by maximum likelihood, not least squares, and there is no analytic solution.
  • It outputs a probability — turning that into a class needs a threshold you choose.

The idea, in plain English
"AI" is not one thing, and the four different ways of classifying it answer four different questions.

By application domain

Natural language processing (understanding, parsing and generating human language); computer vision (interpreting digital images and video); robotics (sensors and algorithms controlling physical or virtual agents); expert systems (mimicking a human expert in a highly constrained domain, such as medical diagnostics).

The nesting that gets confused

Deep learning is inside machine learning, which is inside AI. Symbolic AI is inside AI but outside machine learning — it does not learn from data at all. Reinforcement learning is a kind of machine learning, not an alternative to it.

Connecting it back

Transparency is not a new topic here — it is the trade-off from topic 11 arriving with consequences. Statistical models "make an effort to explain the data generating process"; ML models are "often black-box" with the "focus on prediction accuracy and not explainability".
