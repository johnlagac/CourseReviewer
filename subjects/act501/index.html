ACT 501 · Course reviewer

Language of Business

Financial statements are how a business tells you what happened. This course is about reading that story well enough to act on it — and the exams test whether you can interpret, not whether you can bookkeep.

The one idea behind the whole course
Profit keeps a business attractive. Cash keeps it alive.

Almost every question in this course is a variation on that tension. A company can report record profit and still fail to make payroll. A ratio can improve for a reason you would hate. The course's own roadmap runs business event → accounting equation → financial statements → ratios → analysis → decision, and every topic below is one link in that chain.

You're askingIs this business healthy, and how do I know?
It comparesWhat was earned against what was actually collected, owned and owed.
A good answerNames the number, then says what a manager should do about it.
What the exams look like
  • Midterm — 25%. Covers sessions 1–4: the accounting equation, the four financial statements, liquidity and working capital, long-term assets, the income statement and profitability.
  • Final — 25%. Covers sessions 6–9: debt and capital structure, the statement of cash flows, financial statement analysis, working capital management again, and case-based financial analysis.
  • Both are individual, sat through ALICE with the Respondus LockDown Browser.
  • Question types are mixed: multiple choice, computation, interpretation and short business analysis.
Working capital is examined twice

The rail splits topics into midterm and final by session, but the course intro lists Working Capital Management inside the final's coverage as well. Topics 03 and 04 are the ones this affects — do not treat them as finished once the midterm is behind you.

Explicitly out of scope

A few appear in the professor's worked exercises and are shown where they clarify a decision, but you are never asked to produce one. The accounting equation

MidtermConceptual + direction
The idea, in plain English
Everything a business owns was paid for by somebody — a lender or an owner. The equation just insists you say which.

Think of a car in a driveway. It is worth ₱1,000,000. If ₱600,000 is still owed to the bank, the driver's real stake is ₱400,000. Nothing about the car changed; you have simply split the claim on it in two. A balance sheet does exactly this for a whole company, and it can never not balance, because equity is defined as whatever is left over.

You're askingWho has a claim on this company's resources?
It comparesWhat is owned against what is owed.
It always holdsEvery transaction keeps both sides equal.

The three terms

TermPlain EnglishExamples
AssetsThings the company ownsCash, accounts receivable, inventory, property plant & equipment, intangibles
LiabilitiesThings the company owesLoans, accounts payable, credit card balances
EquityThe owners' claim on what is leftOwner's capital, retained earnings
Assets = Liabilities + Equity rearranged: Equity = Assets − Liabilities

Assets = ₱100,000 and liabilities = ₱40,000.

Equity₱60,000

Say it in plain words

Profit is not a pile of cash sitting somewhere. Profit raises the owners' claim, and that accumulated claim is called retained earnings. Where the cash went is a separate question, answered by a different statement.

The business problem — an ice cream shop

Four transactions, in order. Watch what each one does to the three columns.

TransactionAssetsLiabilitiesEquity
Owner invests ₱100,000 cash+100,0000+100,000
Borrow ₱50,000 from the bank+50,000+50,0000

The trap

Buying equipment for cash changes nothing on any of the three totals. Cash falls ₱30,000, equipment rises ₱30,000 — an asset swap. The company now owns a different mix of assets, not more of them. Exam questions love this one, usually phrased as "what is the effect on total assets?"

Read the answer — direction, not amount

Most exam items about the equation ask only which way each column moves.

TransactionAssetsLiabilitiesEquity
Buy inventory on credit for ₱20,000↑↑—
Pay a supplier ₱5,000 cash↓↓—
Pay employee salaries ₱3,000 cash↓—↓
Make a ₱30,000 sale on credit↑—↑
Collect ₱10,000 of receivables———
Why the last row is all dashes

Collecting a receivable converts one asset into another: receivables down, cash up. No new resource entered the business and nobody's claim changed. The same logic as the equipment purchase, running the other way.

The five account types

Once there are thousands of transactions, everything gets sorted into five buckets. The first three are the equation; the last two explain how equity changed.

The trap

"Unearned revenue" contains the word revenue and is not revenue. Cash arrived before the work was done, so the company owes a service — a liability. The same pairing appears twice above: the cost of utilities used is an expense, the obligation to pay for them is a liability.

Recap
  • Assets = Liabilities + Equity, always, after every transaction.
  • Swaps within one side change nothing — buying equipment for cash, collecting a receivable.
  • Profit raises equity, not cash. Dividends lower equity; borrowing does not touch it.

Session 1

The four financial statements

MidtermFinalConceptual
The idea, in plain English
One business, four questions. Each statement answers exactly one of them.

People treat "the financials" as one document. They are four, and picking the wrong one is the most common way to answer an exam question confidently and wrongly. If the question is about cash, the income statement cannot help you, however profitable the company looks on it.

You're askingWhich statement answers this question?
It comparesPerformance over a period against position at a moment.
They connectProfit flows into equity; cash flows into the balance sheet.

One business, four questions

Executive questionStatementWhat it really tells you
Did we make money?Income statementProfitability over a period
Did equity change?Statement of retained earningsAccumulated wealth kept in the business
What do we own and owe?Balance sheetPosition at a single date
Where did cash go?Cash flow statementLiquidity — actual cash movement

How they link

Income statement: Revenue − Expenses = Net income ↓ Retained earnings: Opening RE + Net income − Dividends = Closing RE ↓ Balance sheet: Assets = Liabilities + (Capital + Closing RE) ↓ Cash flow: Operating ± Investing ± Financing = Change in Cash

Revenue ₱350,000, expenses ₱200,000.

Net income₱150,000

That ₱150,000 does not go to "cash" and it is not "revenue" — it lands in equity, as retained earnings, unless it is paid out as dividends.

Period versus moment

The income statement, retained earnings statement and cash flow statement each cover a stretch of time — "for the year ended". The balance sheet is a photograph — "as at 31 December". That is why ratios mixing the two (ROA, ROE, every turnover ratio) use an average balance sheet figure: you are comparing a year's worth of flow against a position that moved during the year.

Read the answer — why profit is not enough

MetricCompany ACompany B
Revenue₱1B₱1B
Profit₱100M₱100M
Cash₱10M₱500,000
Current liabilities₱20M₱200M
Company B is in trouble

Identical income statements. B owes ₱200M within the year and holds ₱500,000. Reading only the statement that answers "did we make money?" would have told you they were the same company.

Recap
  • Four statements, four different questions. Match the question to the statement before reaching for a number.
  • Net income flows into retained earnings, which flows into the balance sheet. The statements are one system.
  • Balance sheet is a moment; the others are a period. That is why mixed ratios use averages.

Balance sheet & liquidity

MidtermFinalTyped formulas
The idea, in plain English
Profitability asks whether you earned money. Liquidity asks whether you can pay Friday's wages.

A company can be profitable and still die, because revenue is not cash and a customer who has not paid is not a customer who has paid. Liquidity is the short-term survival question, and the balance sheet answers it — but only once you split it into what turns into cash within a year and what does not.

You're askingCan this company survive the next 12 months?
It comparesCurrent assets against current liabilities.
A big value meansMore cushion — but possibly idle resources.

The trap

"More is always better" is wrong, and the exam knows it. Very high working capital can mean uncollected receivables and unsold inventory — assets that are current in name only. The same caution applies to a very high current ratio.

The three liquidity ratios

Working capital gives an absolute amount, which cannot be compared across companies of different sizes. Ratios can.

Current ratio = Current assets / Current liabilities Quick ratio = (Cash + Marketable securities + Accounts receivable) / Current liabilities Cash ratio = (Cash + Cash equivalents) / Current liabilities less conservative → more conservative
RatioWhat it measuresInterpretation
CurrentAbility to pay short-term obligations using all current assetsHigher generally better, but very high may mean idle resources

What counts as a cash equivalent

Extremely low risk and maturing in three months or less. Quick assets are wider: cash, marketable securities and receivables — everything current except inventory and prepaid expenses.

Quick practice. Cash ₱50,000 · accounts receivable ₱70,000 · inventory ₱80,000 · current liabilities ₱100,000.

Lend to Pumba

Recap

  • Working capital is pesos; ratios are comparable. Use the ratio to compare companies, the peso figure to size the cushion.
  • Read the three ratios as a profile. The gaps between them tell you what the current assets actually are.

The cash conversion cycle

MidtermFinalTyped formulas
The idea, in plain English
Count the days between paying your supplier and being paid by your customer. That gap is what you have to finance.

Cash leaves to buy inventory, sits on a shelf, becomes a receivable when it sells, and only comes back when the customer pays. Meanwhile the supplier wants paying. The cash conversion cycle measures how many days you are out of pocket — which is exactly how much working capital you need, and why two equally profitable companies can have completely different financing needs.

You're askingHow long is our cash tied up in operations?
It comparesDays waiting on inventory and customers against days of supplier credit.
A big value meansMore cash trapped, more external financing needed.

The three turnover ratios and their day counts

Receivables: AR turnover = Net credit sales / Average accounts receivable DSO = 365 / AR turnover Inventory: Inv turnover = COGS / Average inventory DIO = 365 / Inventory turnover Payables: AP turnover = COGS / Average accounts payable DPO = 365 / AP turnover Average balance = (Beginning + Ending) / 2
Two conventions this course fixes

Every worked figure in the session 2 deck uses a 365-day year, not 360. And accounts payable turnover uses COGS in the numerator rather than purchases. Some textbooks do it differently; match the course.

Quick practice — receivables. Net credit sales ₱3,000,000 · beginning AR ₱400,000 · ending AR ₱600,000.

Average AR = (400,000 + 600,000) / 2 = 500,000 AR turnover = 3,000,000 / 500,000 = 6.0× DSO = 365 / 6.0 = 60.8 days

Customers take about two months to pay. The company turns its receivables over six times a year.

Long-term assets

MidtermTyped formulas
The idea, in plain English
The price on the invoice is rarely the cost of the asset.

Buy a machine for ₱2,000,000 and you will also pay to ship it, install it and test it. None of that is optional if you want a working machine, so all of it is part of what the machine cost you. Train your staff to use it and that is a different thing — useful, but the machine works whether or not you do it. That single distinction decides the whole topic.

You're askingWhat goes on the balance sheet, and at what number?
The testWas this cost necessary to get the asset ready for use?
Getting it wrongShifts profit between this year and the next several.

What makes an asset long-term

Expected to provide economic benefits beyond one year. Property, plant and equipment is the tangible kind: physical, used in operations, long useful life, not intended for resale.

Interest splits. Interest on a loan to buy equipment is expensed. Interest during the construction period of a building is capitalised — while the asset is being built, financing it is part of getting it ready. Once it is in use, interest is just a financing cost.

Exercise B-16.04 — Fulton Construction

The CFO's conference call, rewritten as data. Everything you need is buried in the prose: net income $10,000,000, net profit margin held at 10%, gross margin 60%, no liabilities at all, opening equity $75,000,000, no new shares, and "5% preferred shareholders" who received their full $2,000,000.

Step 1 — work backwards from the margins.

Net profit margin 10% → Sales = 10,000,000 / 0.10 = 100,000,000 Gross margin 60% → Gross profit = 60% × 100,000,000 = 60,000,000 COGS = 100,000,000 − 60,000,000 = 40,000,000

Step 2 — separate the preferred shares out of equity.

"5% preferred" paying 2,000,000 → Preferred stock = 2,000,000 / 0.05 = 40,000,000 Beginning total equity 75,000,000 Less preferred stock (40,000,000) Beginning COMMON equity 35,000,000 Beginning common equity 35,000,000 Plus net income 10,000,000 Less dividends (2,000,000) Ending common equity 43,000,000 Average common equity = (35,000,000 + 43,000,000) / 2 = 39,000,000

Step 3 — the two returns.

ROE20.51%
ROA12.66%
ROE is higher

Why: ROE here measures the return to common shareholders only, and their capital base — ₱39,000,000 on average — is far smaller than the ₱79,000,000 of assets funding the business. The preferred shareholders supply capital but take a fixed ₱2,000,000; everything the assets earn above that belongs to the common shareholders.

Three traps in one exercise
  • "5% preferred" is a rate, not an amount. The ₱40,000,000 preferred balance comes from dividing the dividend by the rate. Nothing on the page states it directly.
  • "Free of any liabilities" is a gift, not a boast. It tells you assets = equity, which is the only way to get total assets at all.
  • Preferred dividends come out of the ROE numerator and preferred capital out of the denominator. Miss either and the answer changes.

Read the answer — when more profit is worse performance

Net income rose 40%. ROA and ROE both fell. Is this a better-performing company?

Not necessarily

If profit grew 40% while returns fell, the asset and equity base grew faster than 40%. The company raised capital or bought assets and is earning less on every peso invested than it was before. Absolute profit went up; efficiency went down. This is the same shape as the Kahoot item where sales rose 20% and net income only 5% — growth in the numerator tells you nothing until you look at what happened to the denominator.

Recap
  • Margins divide by sales; returns divide by the capital base. Pick the one that matches the question.
  • ROA and ROE use average balances, because they mix a period figure with a point-in-time one.
  • Rising profit with falling returns means the base grew faster — more money invested, less earned on each peso.

Negative is not the same as bad

Negative investing cash flow usually means the company is buying long-term assets — normal, and often a good sign. Negative financing cash flow often means it is repaying debt or paying dividends — also normal. The only section where a minus sign is reliably bad news is operating.   • Interest paid and dividends received are operating; dividends paid are financing. Memorise this asymmetry.
  • Read the pattern of signs. + − − is the healthy shape; − − + is the distress shape.

The idea, in plain English
Three lenses: what changed, where every peso goes, and why the return is what it is.

Two companies both report a return on equity of 18%. Are they equally good? Two basketball players both scored 20 points — one took 15 shots, the other took 30. The totals match; the stories do not. Growth does not always mean better performance

Revenue grew 20% and profit fell. COGS grew faster than sales, so the gross margin was squeezed — the company sold more and kept less of it. This is the exact shape of the Kahoot item "sales increased 20%, net income increased 5%", and the reasonable conclusion is always the same: margin declined.

What horizontal analysis cannot tell you

It says what changed, not whether the amounts are large or small. A 40% rise in an account that is 0.3% of revenue is noise. That is why you also need a common base.

DuPont — why is ROE what it is?

ROE tells you what the return is, not what is driving it. DuPont splits it into three.

ROE = Net profit margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier Net profit margin = Net income / Sales how much profit per peso of sales Asset turnover = Sales / Total assets how efficiently assets generate sales Equity multiplier = Total assets / Equity how much of the assets are debt-funded

Net income ₱10,000,000 · net sales ₱100,000,000 · total assets ₱62,500,000 · shareholders' equity ₱31,250,000.

Net profit margin = 10M / 100M = 10% Asset turnover = 100M / 62.5M = 1.6× Equity multiplier = 62.5M / 31.25M = 2.0× ROE = 10% × 1.6 × 2.0 = 32% Check: 10M / 31.25M = 32% ✓

In words: earns ₱0.10 of profit per ₱1.00 of sales; generates ₱1.60 of sales per ₱1.00 of assets; uses 2× leverage. Those three combine to 32%.

MM is a high-margin, low-volume business — a luxury brand. DD is low-margin and high-volume — a grocery chain. Identical returns, opposite strategies, and completely different things would break them.

The trap, and the Kahoot knows it

Three CEOs each raised ROE: one improved margins, one used assets more efficiently, one borrowed more money. Only the third added risk without improving the business at all — raising the equity multiplier lifts ROE arithmetically while making the company more fragile. When an exam asks which way of increasing ROE carries the greatest financial risk, the answer is higher equity multiplier.

And check the arithmetic before assuming a difference exists: 5% × 2 × 3 = 30% and 10% × 1 × 3 = 30%. The two companies have the same ROE by different routes.

Step 3 — answer the questions

Q1 / Q2 — is it ready for the next stage? Would you invest?

Not on these numbers

Revenue grew 33% and net income 35%, which is the story management will tell. Growth is being financed entirely by debt, and the cash buffer has fallen from ₱1.8 million to ₱900,000, against annual cash operating costs of ₱43.6 million — about a week's worth. The company is not unprofitable; it is outgrowing its cash.

Q3 — which single measure best predicts future performance?

AnswerOperating cash flow

Why it beats the others: net income is positive and rising, so it predicts nothing here. The current ratio improved, so it predicts nothing either. Operating cash flow is the only measure that changed sign, and it is the constraint that will actually bite — a company with negative operating cash flow must keep borrowing to survive, and lenders eventually stop. Say why the alternatives are weaker; that is where the marks are.

Q4 — greatest financial risk?

AnswerRefinancing risk from negative operating cash flow

Not "high debt" on its own — debt-to-assets of 62.7% would be unremarkable in many industries. The risk is the combination: interest coverage falling from 5.64× to 4.89× while the business consumes cash, so each year's shortfall has to be met with more borrowing at higher interest. One credit refusal and operations stop. Name the binding constraint, not the list.

Q7 — which figure is most misleading in isolation?

AnswerReturn on equity, at 26.56%
DuPont: 5.10% × 2.04 × 2.55 = 26.56% margin turnover leverage

A 26.56% ROE looks outstanding. Decomposed, the margin is a thin 5.10% and the equity multiplier is 2.55 — most of that return is borrowed, not earned. The current ratio is a close second: it improved to 2.32 in the year the company nearly ran out of cash, because swelling receivables and inventory count as current assets at full value.

Q8 — improve only ONE metric?

AnswerDays inventory outstanding

At 80.7 days, inventory is the largest component of the 85.4-day cash cycle, and it grew 65% against 33% revenue growth. Cutting DIO to 60 days would release roughly ₱1.8 million of cash — close to the entire operating shortfall — without touching customers or suppliers. Receivables at 46.8 days are already reasonable; stretching payables beyond 42 days risks supply. Quantify the lever, do not just name it.

Q10 — profitability or cash flow?

This company is the argument. It is profitable on every line of the income statement and cannot fund itself. Profit measures whether the business model works; cash measures whether it survives long enough to prove it. For a lender, cash flow, always. For a long-term equity investor, profitability matters more — but only once solvency is not in question, and here it is. The honest answer names the audience before it picks a side.

How these answers are marked

A correct ratio with no recommendation scores poorly. A committed recommendation with a slightly rounded ratio scores well.

  • Growth funded by debt while operations consume cash is the pattern both assigned cases are built around.
  • Commit to a recommendation and name the audience. "It depends" only earns marks when you say what it depends on.
