The nature of statistics

MidtermConceptual
The idea, in plain English
You almost never get to ask everyone. So you ask some, and you argue about the rest.

Every technique in this course exists because a census is impossible, ruinously expensive, or slower than the decision. The whole discipline is a set of rules for saying something defensible about a population you did not fully observe — and for being honest about how wrong you might be.

You're askingWhat is true of the whole group?
You haveA sample — a subset you actually measured.
The dangerA sample that is systematically unlike the population.

Population, sample, census

TermMeaningWatch for
PopulationALL items or individuals relevant to the studyIt is defined, not discovered. Change the definition and you change the answer.
SampleThe subset you actually observeIts value depends entirely on how it was selected.
CensusMeasuring every member of the populationCostly, slow — and often less accurate than a good sample.
Defining the population is a judgement, not a fact

The professor's example: you want to sample 500 listeners of an FM station specialising in music. What is the population? Listeners to that station in general? Listeners to its classical programme? Only regular listeners? All are defensible, and they give different answers. In medical trials the population is often "patients who arrive at the hospital running the trial" — which quietly excludes everyone too poor, too remote, or too sick to travel.

This is why official statistics spend so long defining terms. There is no universal consensus on what "unemployed" means, and the definition drives the number.

Bias

In ordinary English, bias means prejudice. In statistics it has a precise meaning you meet properly in Topic 5: an estimator is unbiased if its expected value equals the parameter it estimates. For now, the practical meaning is what matters.

Systematic bias is not fixed by a bigger sample

Random error shrinks as n grows. Systematic bias does not — it just gets measured more precisely. A survey of internet users about internet access will be exactly as wrong with 10,000 responses as with 100.

Why sample at all?

Argument for a censusArgument for a sample
No sampling error at allFar cheaper and faster — the decision may not wait
Complete sub-group detailFewer units means each can be measured more carefully
Politically defensibleNon-response and recording errors are easier to control
Can a sample beat a census? Yes.

A census is enormous, so it is executed by many people under time pressure, with high non-response and heavy data-entry error. A 1,000-unit sample can be run by trained staff who chase every non-respondent. Total error is sampling error plus non-sampling error, and a census only removes the first while inflating the second.

Four ways to choose the sample

MethodHowNeeds a sample frame?Sampling error measurable?
Simple randomEvery unit has an equal, known, non-zero chanceYesYes

Sample frame

A sample frame is the list from which you draw — an electoral roll, a customer database, a register of firms. Random sampling is impossible without one. The frame's flaws become the sample's flaws: if it is out of date, the mobile and the newly arrived are silently excluded.

Stratified versus quota — the exam's favourite contrast

They can produce identically balanced samples and are still fundamentally different. Stratified selects randomly within each stratum, so probabilities are known and sampling error can be computed. Quota lets the interviewer choose whoever fills the quota — so people who are easy to approach are over-represented, and no valid standard error, confidence interval or significance test can be computed.

Quota's advantage is real: it is fast, cheap, and needs no frame. It is the right tool when no frame exists — you just cannot attach inferential statistics to it.

Observational studies versus designed experiments

Observational studyDesigned experiment
Who sets the factor levelsNobody — you watch what happensThe investigator
Typical settingSurveys, economics, marketingIndustrial, agricultural, clinical
ConfoundingAlways a live threatBroken by randomised allocation
Strongest claimAssociationCausation

This distinction returns in force in Topic 9, where control groups and time order are the machinery that turns association into a causal claim.

Recap
  • The population is a definition you choose, and different defensible definitions give different answers. State it before you sample.
  • Random sampling needs a frame; quota sampling does not — and that is exactly why quota samples cannot carry a confidence interval.
  • Only a designed experiment licenses a causal claim. Observational data gets you association and a list of plausible confounders.

Data presentation

MidtermTyped formulas
The idea, in plain English
Two numbers — a middle and a spread — plus one picture, and you have said most of what a dataset has to say.

Summarising is lossy on purpose. The skill is knowing which summary throws away the right information for the question, and which one quietly misleads because the data is skewed or has an outlier.

You're askingWhat does this data look like?
It gives youA centre, a spread, and a shape.
A big spread meansThe centre alone is a poor description.

Measures of location

Worked: both mean formulas agree

Hours of television watched per week by 10 people: 2, 2, 2, 5, 5, 10, 10, 10, 10, 10.

Ungrouped: (2+2+2+5+5+10+10+10+10+10) / 10 = 66 / 10 = 6.6 Grouped: value 2 → f=3, value 5 → f=2, value 10 → f=5 Σgf = (2×3) + (5×2) + (10×5) = 6 + 10 + 50 = 66 Σf = 10 x̄ = 66 / 10 = 6.6
Mean6.6 hours

The grouped formula is not a different mean. It is the same sum with the equal values collected together.

The median is not always in the data

If n is odd the median is the middle observation. If n is even it is the average of the two middle values — so the median of 1, 3, 4, 5 is 3.5, a value that never occurs. The mode, by contrast, need not be unique: 1, 2, 2, 3, 5, 9, 9, 11 has two modes, 2 and 9.

Measures of dispersion

Population variance σ² = (1/n) Σ(xᵢ − μ)² Sample variance s² = 1/(n−1) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² ← divide by n−1, always, for a sample Computing form s² = 1/(n−1) [ Σxᵢ² − (Σxᵢ)²/n ] Standard deviation s = √s² MAD (1/n) Σ|xᵢ − x̄| Range max − min Interquartile range IQR = Q3 − Q1
Why n − 1?

Dividing by n gives a biased estimate that systematically understates the population variance, because deviations are taken about the sample mean rather than the true mean. The correction is derived in Topic 5. For now: population → divide by n; sample → divide by n − 1.

Say it in plain words

Mean above median → long tail to the right. Mean below median → long tail to the left. When they diverge noticeably, quote the median.

Choosing the picture

Every diagram needs a title, labelled axes and units. The professor lists this as a learning objective in its own right. Also: spreadsheets routinely use the wrong terminology — Excel calls a bar chart a "column chart" and will happily draw a "histogram" with gaps.

Recap
  • Mean uses all the information and pays for it in outlier sensitivity. When mean and median diverge, the data is skewed and the median is the honest summary.
  • Sample variance divides by n − 1, population variance by n. The computing form Σx² − (Σx)²/n is faster and less error-prone by hand.
  • Histogram bars touch and encode frequency by area; pie charts cap at four or five slices. Label the axes and state the units.

Probability

MidtermTyped formulas
The idea, in plain English
Probability is bookkeeping for uncertainty. The rules are few; the difficulty is translating the sentence.

Nearly every mistake in this topic is a reading mistake, not an arithmetic one — confusing "and" with "or", or confusing P(A given B) with P(B given A). Get the translation right and the axioms do the rest.

You're askingHow likely is this outcome?
It needsA sample space and an event within it.
A high value meansThe event occupies much of the space.

Sample space and events

SymbolNameEnglishDie example, E = {3,4}, F = {4,5,6}
SSample spaceEvery possible outcome{1,2,3,4,5,6}
E ∪ FUnioneither, or both{3,4,5,6}
E ∩ FIntersectionand{4}
EᶜComplementnot{1,2,5,6}
Outcomes can themselves be sets

Sampling two people without replacement from {Mabel, Belmont, Gertrude, Elsie} gives a sample space of six pairs. The event "the sample includes Gertrude" is the subset of three pairs containing her. This matters in Topic 4, where the sampling distribution is built on exactly this idea.

The three axioms

1. P(E) ≥ 0 probabilities are never negative 2. P(S) = 1 something must happen 3. P(E ∪ F) = P(E) + P(F) IF E ∩ F = ∅ (mutually exclusive) General addition rule (overlapping events): P(E ∪ F) = P(E) + P(F) − P(E ∩ F) Complement: P(Eᶜ) = 1 − P(E)

Conditional probability and independence

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) defined only when P(B) > 0 A and B are independent ⟺ P(A | B) = P(A) ⟺ P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B)
P(A|B) is not P(B|A)

The probability you have spots given you have measles is close to 1. The probability you have measles given you have spots is small. Reversing a conditional is the single most consequential error in applied probability — it is the same error behind misread medical tests and misread false-positive rates.

Independent ≠ mutually exclusive

They are nearly opposites. If A and B are mutually exclusive and both have non-zero probability, then knowing A occurred tells you B definitely did not — so they are maximally dependent. Mutually exclusive gives P(A ∩ B) = 0; independent gives P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B) > 0.

Worked: probability tree, two suppliers

60% of parts come from Manufacturer A (97% good), the rest from Z (93% good).

Multiply along a path, add across paths. That sentence solves most tree questions.

Worked: a system that survives one failure

Four independent devices, each failing with probability 0.1. The system works if at least one of A or B works and at least one of C or D works.

P(A or B works) = 1 − P(both fail) = 1 − (0.1 × 0.1) = 0.99 P(C or D works) = 0.99 P(system works) = 0.99 × 0.99 = 0.9801
P(system works)0.9801

"At least one" is almost always easier as 1 − P(none). Redundancy takes a 10% failure rate down to 1%.

Counting, for the harder questions

The supplementary handout covers arrangements. Order matters → permutations, ⁿPᵣ = n!/(n−r)!. Order does not → combinations, ⁿCᵣ = n!/(r!(n−r)!). In Excel: =PERMUT(n,r) and =COMBIN(n,r).

Recap
  • "And" divides by the grand total; "given" divides by the row total. Identify which the sentence is asking before you compute anything.
  • P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A), and independent is not mutually exclusive — the latter pair are closer to opposites.
  • On a tree, multiply along a path and add across paths; the complete set of paths must sum to 1. For "at least one", use 1 − P(none).

The normal distribution, sampling and the CLT

MidtermFinalTyped formulas
Source note

The Session 5 slide deck is a scanned image with no readable text, so this topic is built from the professor's Session 5 notes. Worked examples that existed only on those slides are not reproduced here.

The idea, in plain English
Individual things vary wildly. Averages of things barely vary at all — and they vary in a shape we know exactly.

This is the hinge of the whole course. Everything before it describes data; everything after it makes claims about populations. The bridge is that x̄ has a known distribution centred on μ, with a spread that shrinks predictably as the sample grows.

You're askingHow does a sample mean behave?
It comparesAn observation against a known curve.
A big z meansFar from the centre; unusual.

Random variables, expectation and variance

E[X] = Σ xᵢ p(xᵢ) another name for μ Var[X] = E[(X − E[X])²] another name for σ² = E[X²] − (E[X])² the computing form

The normal distribution

A normal random variable is completely determined by two numbers: its mean μ and variance σ². We write X ~ N(μ, σ²). Its usefulness is not that data is often normal — it is that averages are, whatever the parent distribution.

Standardise: Z = (X − μ) / σ converts any N(μ, σ²) into N(0, 1) Symmetry: Φ(−k) = 1 − Φ(k) Excel: =NORM.S.DIST(z, TRUE) left-tail area for a z value =NORM.DIST(x, μ, σ, TRUE) left-tail area without standardising =NORM.S.INV(p) the z value with left-tail area p

Worked: men's heights

Heights are N(68, 3²) inches. Find the proportion of men under 66, over 72, and between.

z(66) = (66 − 68)/3 = −0.67 z(72) = (72 − 68)/3 = 1.33 P(X < 66) = Φ(−0.67) = 1 − Φ(0.67) = 1 − 0.7454 = 0.2546 P(X > 72) = 1 − Φ(1.33) = 1 − 0.9082 = 0.0918 P(66 < X < 72) = 1 − 0.2546 − 0.0918 = 0.6536

Always sketch the curve and shade the region. Most errors here are tail-direction errors, and a sketch catches every one of them.

The empirical rule — worth memorising

Roughly 68% within 1σ of the mean, 95% within 2σ, 99.7% within 3σ. Useful as a sanity check: if your computed tail area implies something wildly off these, you have made a sign error.

Sampling distributions

Take every possible sample of size n, compute x̄ for each, and collect those values. That collection is the sampling distribution of the mean — and it is a completely different thing from the distribution of the underlying data.

E[X̄] = μ always, with or without replacement With replacement: Var[X̄] = σ²/n Without replacement: Var[X̄] = (σ²/n) · (N−n)/(N−1) ← finite population correction Standard error: SE = σ/√n

Worked: the four-family population

Four families have 1, 2, 3 and 3 children. Population mean μ = 9/4 = 2.25; population variance σ² = 0.6875. Take samples of size 2.

Without replacement (6 equally likely pairs): Var[X̄] = (0.6875 / 2) × (4 − 2)/(4 − 1) = 0.34375 × 0.6667 = 0.2292 With replacement (16 ordered outcomes): Var[X̄] = 0.6875 / 2 = 0.34375 Both give E[X̄] = 2.25 = μ

Sampling without replacement is more precise — you cannot waste a draw re-measuring the same unit. The correction factor (N−n)/(N−1) is negligible when the population is large relative to the sample, which is why it is usually dropped.

The Central Limit Theorem

Say it in plain words

For a large enough sample, the sampling distribution of x̄ is approximately normal with mean μ and variance σ²/n — no matter what shape the population has. That is why normal-based methods work on skewed business data such as incomes, waiting times and claim sizes.

P( (X̄ − μ) / (σ/√n) ≤ x ) → Φ(x) as n → ∞
What the CLT does not say
  • It does not say the data becomes normal. The raw observations keep their original skew forever.
  • It does not help with a single observation — only with means and sums.
  • Quadrupling n halves the standard error, because of the square root. Precision is expensive: each extra decimal place costs a hundredfold increase in sample size.
Recap
  • Standardise with z = (x − μ)/σ, then read a tail area. Sketch and shade before computing — tail-direction errors are the common failure.
  • E[X̄] = μ and SE = σ/√n. The sample mean is unbiased, and its spread shrinks with the square root of n, not with n.
  • The CLT makes x̄ approximately normal whatever the population looks like — this licenses every confidence interval and test that follows.

Estimation and confidence intervals

MidtermFinalTyped formulas
The idea, in plain English
A single number is a guess. A guess with an interval around it is a finding.

Estimates from samples are necessarily imprecise, so reporting one number alone hides the thing a decision-maker most needs: how wrong it might plausibly be. A confidence interval attaches that honesty to the estimate.

You're askingWhat is the population value, roughly?
It gives youA range, plus a confidence level.
A wide interval meansSmall sample, or a variable population.

Estimators, and what makes one good

A statistic T is an UNBIASED estimator of θ if E[T] = θ for every θ. x̄ is unbiased for μ. (1/n)Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² is BIASED for σ² — it understates by σ²/n. 1/(n−1) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² is unbiased. ← this is why we divide by n − 1

That is the promised answer from Topic 2: dividing by n − 1 is not a fudge, it is the correction that makes the estimator unbiased.

The interval, and which distribution to use

σ KNOWN (or n large): x̄ ± z_{α/2} · σ/√n σ ESTIMATED by s: x̄ ± t_{α/2, n−1} · s/√n z values you should know cold: 90% → 1.645 95% → 1.96 99% → 2.576 (≈ 2.58) Excel: =NORM.S.INV(0.975) → 1.95996 =T.INV.2T(0.05, df) → two-tailed t critical value =CONFIDENCE.NORM(alpha, sigma, n) / =CONFIDENCE.T(alpha, s, n)
The decision rule, stated once

Use the normal when the variance is given to you — as a population figure or stated by the researcher. Use Student's t whenever you estimated the variance from the sample, regardless of sample size. For large n, t converges on the normal anyway, so following this rule never costs you anything. The professor's convention is that beyond about ν = 30, the normal is an acceptable substitute.

200 ball bearings, sample mean diameter 0.824, population σ = 0.042.

Higher confidence buys a wider interval. You cannot become more certain and more precise at the same time without collecting more data.

Worked: small sample, σ estimated

The incorrect interval is narrower, which is exactly why the error is dangerous — it manufactures a false sense of accuracy. With s estimated from only 10 observations, you genuinely know less, and t widens the interval to reflect that.

Intervals for proportions

Substitute into the same structure: μ → Π (population proportion) x̄ → p (sample proportion) σ/√n → √( p(1−p)/n ) ← the standard error of a proportion CI: p ± z_{α/2} · √( p(1−p)/n )

Worked: voter poll

100 voters sampled; 55% favour the candidate.

95% CI(0.452, 0.648)
The managerial reading

The 95% interval includes 0.50. Despite a headline "55% support", this poll cannot establish that the candidate has majority support. A margin of error of roughly ±10 points is what n = 100 buys you.

Differences between two groups

Difference of means: (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ± z_{α/2} · √( s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂ ) Difference of proportions: (p_A − p_B) ± z_{α/2} · √( p_A(1−p_A)/n_A + p_B(1−p_B)/n_B )

Worked: comparing two holiday companies

Company A: 220 of 300 satisfied. Company B: 200 of 250 satisfied.

p_A = 220/300 = 0.7333 p_B = 200/250 = 0.8000 difference = 0.7333 − 0.8000 = −0.0667 SE = √( 0.7333×0.2667/300 + 0.8×0.2/250 ) = √( 0.000652 + 0.000640 ) = √0.001292 = 0.03594 95% CI: −0.0667 ± 1.96 × 0.03594 → (−0.1371, +0.0038)
95% CI for the difference(−0.137, +0.004)

The interval contains zero, so on this evidence you cannot conclude that either company gives more satisfaction. B looks better in the raw percentages, but the gap is within what sampling variation alone would produce.

How large a sample do I need?

Set the half-width to the error you can tolerate and solve for n: z·σ/√n = E → n = ( z·σ / E )² then ROUND UP Reaction times, σ = 0.05 s, want error ≤ 0.01 s: 95%: n = (1.96 × 0.05 / 0.01)² = 96.04 → 97 observations 99%: n = (2.58 × 0.05 / 0.01)² = 166.41 → 167 observations
Always round up

96.04 rounds to 97, not 96. Rounding down leaves the error above your tolerance, which defeats the purpose of the calculation.

What "95% confident" actually means

It is a statement about the procedure, not about this one interval. If you repeatedly drew samples of the same size and built an interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true parameter. The parameter is fixed; the interval is what varies. So "there is a 95% probability μ lies in (0.818, 0.830)" is the wrong phrasing — μ either is or is not in there.

This is directly examinable: given 500 samples each yielding a 99% interval, the expected number containing μ is 500 × 0.99 = 495.

Recap
  • σ given → z. σ estimated from the sample → t, whatever n is. Using z when you should use t produces a falsely narrow interval.
  • An interval containing zero (for a difference) means "no significant difference". The interval and the hypothesis test always agree.
  • "95% confident" describes the procedure across repeated samples, not the probability that this particular interval is right.

Hypothesis testing

MidtermFinal · ToolpakTyped formulas
The idea, in plain English
Assume nothing interesting is happening. Then ask how embarrassing your data would be if that were true.

A hypothesis test never proves anything. It measures surprise. If the data would be very unusual under the "nothing is happening" assumption, you abandon that assumption — while accepting a known rate of being wrong.

You're askingIs this difference real, or noise?
It comparesObserved data against H₀.

Setting up the hypotheses

H₀ : the status quo, no change, no difference ← ALWAYS an equality H₁ : what you would need evidence to believe One-sided: H₁ : θ > θ₀ or H₁ : θ < θ₀ → one-tailed test Two-sided: H₁ : θ ≠ θ₀ → two-tailed test
The direction comes from the wording, never from the data

Decide one- or two-tailed before looking at which way the sample went. "Is it higher than…" is one-tailed. "Is there a difference…" is two-tailed. Choosing the tail after seeing the result doubles your true error rate.

Careful with negations. "Test the claim that at least half of shoppers can state the price" means H₀: p ≥ 0.5, so H₁: p < 0.5 — the whole rejection region sits in the left tail.

The two ways to be wrong

H₀ is actually trueH₀ is actually false
You do not reject H₀Correct — probability 1 − αType II error — probability β
You reject H₀Type I error — probability αCorrect — power = 1 − β
In words
  • Type I — crying wolf. You announce an effect that is not there. Rate = α, which you choose.
  • Type II — missing the wolf. A real effect goes undetected. Rate = β.
  • Power = 1 − β — the chance of detecting an effect that genuinely exists.

α and β trade off: shrinking α to 0.01 makes Type I rarer and Type II more likely. The only way to improve both at once is a bigger sample.

The Activity 7.3 chart labels (1 − α) "the confidence interval of the test". It is the confidence level; a confidence interval is a range of parameter values, which is a different object entirely. The table above is the corrected version.

β and power, computed

  1. Find the cut-off value of x̄ at which you would just reject H₀, using the null mean.
  2. Standardise that cut-off against the true mean supplied by the question.

The counting questions

Two recurring exam types with one-line answers. "500 samples, each a 99% CI — how many contain μ?" → 500 × 0.99 = 495.   • Type I = α = crying wolf; Type II = β = missing the wolf; power = 1 − β. Shrinking α raises β; only a bigger sample improves both.
  • Same subjects → paired test. For a proportion test, the standard error uses p₀, not p̂.

The idea, in plain English
Every technique in this course assumes the data arrived honestly. This topic is about all the ways it doesn't.

The professor's example

You want 20 boys and 20 girls from a register of 200 boys and 100 girls. You sample 1/10 of the boys and 1/5 of the girls. Selection chances are known but unequal — so this is a random sample but not a simple random one. Stratified and cluster samples are both forms of random sampling, and inference works for all of them.

The asymmetry that matters

Sampling error can be quantified in advance — it is the outcome of a planning decision about how much accuracy you are buying. Non-sampling error is nearly undetectable after the fact and arises from trivial-looking causes: one word misunderstood by less-educated respondents, one interviewer's manner, a lost batch of questionnaires.

The professor's example of response error: UK Family Expenditure Survey alcohol consumption is understated by up to 50% against known sales figures. Every respondent answered; they simply did not answer truthfully.

Frame problems, non-response, response bias

  • Frame — a list of householders misses people who just moved in; a list of over-18s misses young people careless about registering.
  • Non-response — the question is never "how many refused" but "what do the refusers have in common?" If they share a characteristic, the bias is systematic.
  • Response error — a clear answer built on a misunderstanding, or a deliberate misstatement. Hardest of all to detect.
The pilot survey earns its keep twice

It estimates the standard errors you need to choose a sample size, and it surfaces non-sampling problems: do people understand the questionnaire, are the interviewers working well, are there organisational problems. Government surveys sometimes add a post-enumeration survey — re-interviewing a subsample to check they understood and answered correctly.

When quota sampling is the right call

Quota is not simply "worse". It is the correct choice when:

  • You are in a hurry — an interviewer filling a quota is far faster than one chasing a named individual.
  • No usable list exists — you cannot legally obtain a list of credit-card debtors, and bus-company employee lists are fragmented and confidential.
  • Cost must come down.
  • Detailed accuracy is not needed — you want a general read on whether people like the new wrapper, not a number with a confidence interval.
The two quota dangers
  • Non-respondents vanish. Quota counts only those who reply. If everyone who disliked the new wrapper avoided the interviewer, the result is badly misleading — and nothing in the data reveals it. Good practice (and the British Market Research Association's rule) is to record non-response even in quota samples.
  • Adding controls erodes the advantage. Tightening from "women aged 25–34" to "mothers of young children" reduces bias but costs time — and once you demand age, sex, employment status, marital status and childbearing stage, you have lost the cost saving and created a strong incentive for interviewers to cheat.

  • Stratify to cut standard error; cluster to cut cost. Clustering usually costs precision through intra-class correlation.
  • Sampling error is planned and quantifiable; non-sampling error is neither — and a pilot survey is the main defence against it.

Causation and association

FinalConceptual
The idea, in plain English
In a laboratory you change one thing and watch. In business, forty things change at once and your rival is one of them.

No calculation in this course establishes causation. What establishes it — imperfectly — is design: a control group that isolates the treatment, and time order that rules out the effect preceding the cause.

You're askingDid X actually cause Y?
It needsA control group and time order.
Without themYou have association and a confounder list.

The munchy bar campaign — why ceteris paribus fails in business

You measure sales of bar A in areas X and Y, launch bar B with a new wrapper, and run a four-month campaign. In area X, B far outsells A. In area Y, B does worse.

Investigation reveals that during the campaign, your main rival withdrew from area X and concentrated on area Y. Your "success" in X measures your competitor's retreat, not your advertising.

Nothing was wrong with the arithmetic. The design could not separate the treatment from everything else that moved.

Weapon one — the control group

When experimentation is impossible for ethical or practical reasons, you look for a naturally occurring comparison and match on everything else that matters.

The fluoride problem

You cannot ethically add fluoride to a town's water to see what happens to tooth decay. But fluoride occurs naturally in some communities — so compare. Before you trust the result, ask whether the two communities match on dentists per person, sweet shops per person, eating habits, and age distribution. If they match on everything except fluoride, the comparison means something.

Where experimentation is permitted, you can separate an experimental group (received the treatment) from a control group (did not), and add:

  • Blind — the subject does not know which group they are in.
  • Double-blind — neither the subject nor the person assessing the outcome knows.
  • Placebo — the control receives an inert treatment, so expectation affects both groups equally.

Weapon two — time order

A cause must precede its effect. Establishing the order is what longitudinal and panel studies are for: the same individuals re-surveyed over time.

The NCDS — the professor's worked case

The UK National Child Development Study has followed everyone born in England, Scotland and Wales in one week of March 1958 — 17,415 babies, 98% of all births that week. It began as a study of childhood health and nutrition by social group and now, with the cohort in their sixties, informs research on retirement and ageing.

Smoking and lung cancer — three explanations one table cannot separate

Researchers compared hospital patients diagnosed with lung cancer against a control group without it (accident cases, appendicitis, pregnancy), each pair matched on age, sex, occupation, home area, and being in hospital at roughly the same time. The main difference turned out to be smoking. They had expected environmental pollution.

But descriptive statistics alone cannot distinguish:

(a) smoking → lung cancer smoking causes it (b) lung cancer → smoking smoking is an early symptom (c) factor V → smoking + lung cancer a third factor drives both

The researchers then ran a longitudinal study of 40,000 UK doctors, collecting smoking histories and tracking subsequent lung-cancer deaths. Time order now runs the right way, and the findings held.

Even so, it is not proof. Heavy smokers might share some other characteristic that causes the disease. You cannot ethically assign people to smoke heavily, so a pure experiment is impossible — which is precisely why this case is the standard illustration.

The caveat sentence, for the exam

Any conclusion from observational data — a chi-square test, a correlation, a regression — should end with something like: "This establishes association, not causation; a third factor such as [name a plausible one] could drive both." Naming a specific confounder rather than reciting the slogan is what separates a good answer from a memorised one.

Recap
  • Statistics never establishes causation; design does. The two instruments are the control group and time order.
  • Matching makes a control group work — match on everything relevant, then compare on the one factor that differs.
  • Longitudinal and panel studies buy time order at the cost of drop-out and conditioning. Association plus a named confounder is usually the strongest honest claim.

F.INV.RT versus F.INV

F.INV.RT(α, df1, df2) gives the value with area α in the right tail — which is what you want, because F tests are right-tailed. F.INV works from the left tail and will hand you the wrong number. Getting the two df arguments the wrong way round is the other common slip; they are not interchangeable.

Worked: recovering F from a regression's t

A simple regression reports a slope t Stat of 3.2892. What is the F statistic?

The F-test for two variances

Put the larger variance in Variable 1

That makes F > 1 and puts you in the right tail, which is where the critical values live. Use this test either to answer a consistency question in its own right ("are the two routes equally reliable?") or as the step before choosing a t-test — it tells you whether to assume equal or unequal variances in Topic 6.

A practical rule of thumb

If one variance is within roughly a factor of three of the other, the equal-variances t-test is fine. A 25-fold gap — say 7.9 against 193.9 — clearly calls for the unequal-variances (Welch) version, which adjusts the degrees of freedom to compensate.

Where else F shows up
  • Regression output — the ANOVA block's F and Significance F test whether the model explains anything.
  • One-way ANOVA — F = between-group variance ÷ within-group variance. See Topic 12.
  • Choosing between the two-sample t-tests — as above.
Recap
  • F is a variance ratio: non-negative, right-skewed, two degrees of freedom. Always a right-tailed test.
  • =F.INV.RT(alpha, df1, df2) — alpha first, numerator df second. Larger variance goes in Variable 1.
  • In simple regression, F = t².

Multiple regression

Worked: urban property tax rates

Twenty US cities. Standard errors in parentheses.

ŷ = 1.79 + 0.000567 x₁ + 0.0183 x₂ − 0.000191 x₃ (0.000139) (0.0082) (0.000446) x₁ = housing units per square mile x₂ = % of city revenue from state and federal grants x₃ = median per capita personal income ($) df = n − k − 1 = 20 − 3 − 1 = 16 t(0.025, 16) = 2.120 CI for x₁ : 0.000567 ± 2.120 × 0.000139 → ( 0.000272, 0.000862) CI for x₂ : 0.0183 ± 2.120 × 0.0082 → (+0.000916, +0.035684) CI for x₃ : −0.000191 ± 2.120 × 0.000446 → (−0.001137, +0.000755)
Reading it
  • The intervals for x₁ and x₂ exclude zero — both are significant at 5%.
  • The interval for x₃ contains zero, so median income is not statistically significant. Even its direction is uncertain.
  • The coefficient 0.0183 means a one-percentage-point rise in grant share raises the effective tax rate by 0.0183, holding the other predictors constant. That clause is what separates multiple from simple regression — and it is where the marks are.
Significance F tiny, one P-value large — not a contradiction

Significance F asks "do the predictors jointly explain Y?" Each row's P-value asks "does this one add anything the others have not already provided?" A weak predictor alongside two strong ones produces exactly this pattern. The CI and the t-test always agree with each other, so you can answer from either.

R² versus Adjusted R²

R² never falls when you add a predictor, even a useless one. Adjusted R² penalises extra predictors, so it is the honest comparison between models with different numbers of terms. But when a question says "fraction of the variation explained", it means R Square.

Logistic regression

What it is for

When the outcome is a category rather than a number — will the customer churn, will the loan default, will the applicant be approved — a linear regression is the wrong tool: it happily predicts probabilities above 1 and below 0. Logistic regression models the log-odds instead, so the fitted value is always a valid probability between 0 and 1. A positive coefficient raises the odds of the outcome; a negative one lowers them.

One-way ANOVA

Data→Data Analysis→ANOVA: Single Factor
H₀ : μ₁ = μ₂ = … = μ_k all group means are equal H₁ : at least one differs NOT "all differ" F = between-group variance ÷ within-group variance df = (k − 1, N − k) k = groups, N = total observations

Worked: fog index across three magazines

Reading-difficulty index for Scientific American, Fortune and the New Yorker — six observations each.

k = 3 groups, N = 18 observations df between = k − 1 = 3 − 1 = 2 df within = N − k = 18 − 3 = 15 df total = N − 1 = 17 ← check: 2 + 15 = 17 ✓
df2 and 15

Select all three group columns as one block, Grouped By Columns. Then compare the P-value to α.

Two things to get right after you reject
  • Rejecting H₀ shows only that at least one mean differs from at least one other. It does not say all differ, and it does not identify which — that needs a post-hoc test. Claiming more is a classic overreach.
  • Read the group means before writing anything. The ANOVA gives you significance; the means tell you the direction and the story.
Why not three t-tests?

Three tests at 5% each give roughly a 14% chance of at least one false positive. ANOVA asks the question once and holds the overall error rate at 5%. That error-rate control is the entire justification.

Out of scope

Two-way ANOVA (two factors at once, with interaction) appears on the slides, and the professor states plainly: "Two-way ANOVA will not be in the Final Exam." Recognise the table; do not drill it.
